\section{Representative HealthGAN Sample}
\label{sec:results:hg-sample}

One preliminary point fixes the HealthGAN operating point used throughout
this chapter. Unlike the other generators, the HealthGAN pipeline produces
multiple interchangeable synthetic draws from the same fitted model in a
single run.
A single representative draw (draw~$0$) is used in every HealthGAN result
reported below. As established in Section~\ref{sec:methods:healthgan:variants}, 
the fidelity spread across the retained draws is negligible on both the marginal 
and the joint axes. This is shown in Table~\ref{tab:hg-variants}. 
The principal-component projections of the different draws also overlap almost
exactly, as shown in Appendix~\ref{app:hg-variants}. Since the HealthGAN
samples produced very similar results, the following conclusions about
HealthGAN are not sensitive to which sample was used. The representative sample
was fixed before the cross-method comparison, so it was not chosen to make
HealthGAN appear better on any metric.

\section{RQ1 - Methods and Metrics for Evaluating Synthetic Medical Data}
\label{sec:results:rq1}

% RQ1 asks which methods and metrics are suitable for evaluating the utility
% and privacy risk of synthetic tabular medical data, and what distinct
% dimension each captures. This section answers that question before the
% generator rankings of RQ2 and RQ3.

\subsection{Full Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:results:rq1:operationalising}

The framework used to answer RQ1 is not a single score, but a layered
protocol. At the top level, the thesis separates \emph{utility} from
\emph{privacy}. Utility is divided into statistical similarity and
predictive performance, because a synthetic table can reproduce marginal
distributions while still failing as training data for a downstream
model. Privacy is evaluated separately through distance-based, inference-based,
and outlier/linkability analyses. The full operational definitions are given in
Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation}; this section explains why the
components are jointly necessary for answering RQ1.

The full protocol contains both headline single-number metrics and
supporting diagnostics. The headline metrics are used in the comprehensive
summary table and aggregate views. The supporting diagnostics, including
per-column marginal plots, mean and standard-deviation checks, ROC curves,
feature-importance profiles, Cox hazard-ratio plots, and PCA projections,
are retained because they explain why a headline metric changes and expose
failure modes that a scalar can hide.

Concretely, the comprehensive summary table
(Table~\ref{tab:rq2:summary}) reports the twelve headline scalars drawn
from the instruments catalogued in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-coverage}: it is a
compact scalar subset of that framework, not a separate set of metrics.
The instruments absent from the summary fall into two groups. The first
is inherently visual and has no single value to tabulate (marginal plots,
correlation heatmaps, ROC curves, PCA projections, and the aggregate
views themselves). The second is scalar but already covered by a stronger
summary on the same axis: the mean and standard-deviation checks are
subsumed by the more shape-sensitive mean KS. NNAA privacy loss is
retained because it complements membership-inference advantage: NNAA
measures nearest-neighbour train-test resemblance, whereas membership
inference measures attack-based train/non-member distinguishability.
Reporting both in the headline table shows that the negligible privacy
concern is consistent across two memorisation probes.

The framework therefore contains deliberate overlap, but not unnecessary
duplication. For example, AUC-ROC and F1 both evaluate downstream
classification, but they answer different questions: AUC-ROC measures
threshold-independent ranking quality, while F1 exposes behaviour at the
chosen decision threshold. Likewise, nearest-neighbour distance and NNDR
both measure proximity, but NNDR makes that proximity interpretable
relative to the real data's own spacing. Membership-inference AUC and
membership-inference advantage report the same attack on two scales; the
advantage form is retained because it is the directly interpretable excess
over random guessing.

\subsection{Empirical Usefulness on the Benchmark}
\label{sec:results:rq1:empirical}

The empirical results in Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2} show that the
metrics were not equally discriminative. The strongest separation came
from the marginal and joint similarity metrics like TSTR AUC-ROC,
nearest-neighbour distance ratio, attribute-inference and outlier
linkability audits. Mean KS, PCD, and PCA projection separated the
off-manifold PATE-GAN output from the four faithful generators, while PCD
also distinguished the stronger joint-structure preservation of
Pythia-1B. TSTR AUC-ROC provided the clearest predictive-utility ordering
across all five generators and made the LLM-side differential-privacy cost
visible.

Several diagnostics were less decisive for ranking but important for
interpretation. Per-column plots explained why PATE-GAN's scalar
similarity scores failed. ROC curves showed that the AUC ordering was not
an artefact of a single operating point. Feature-importance and Cox
agreement checked whether predictive utility was supported by comparable
predictor structure and clinically interpretable effect estimates.
Membership inference and NNAA privacy loss were nearly flat across
generators, but they are still useful safeguards: their near-zero values
support the conclusion that the observed privacy differences are not driven
by direct training-set memorisation. Attribute inference, by contrast,
identified the clearest privacy benefit of the differentially private LLM
variant, since it removed the mild primary-diagnosis leakage observed for
Pythia-1B.

\subsection{Recommended Minimal Evaluation Suite}
\label{sec:results:rq1:minimal-suite}

The minimal suite is not intended to replace the full evaluation protocol
used in this thesis. The full protocol remains necessary for validation,
interpretation, and sensitivity checks. The minimal suite is instead a
practical recommendation for future studies that need a compact first-pass
evaluation while still covering the main utility and privacy failure
modes.

For such a compact evaluation, the recommended core metrics are:
mean KS, pairwise correlation difference, TSTR AUC-ROC, median NNDR,
membership-inference advantage, maximum attribute-inference advantage, and
outlier linkability. Mean KS and PCD cover marginal and joint statistical
fidelity, TSTR AUC-ROC covers downstream predictive utility, median NNDR
captures scale-normalised record proximity, membership-inference advantage
tests aggregate training-set leakage, attribute-inference advantage tests
conditional sensitive-attribute leakage, and outlier linkability tests
tail-record disclosure risk. Membership-inference advantage is retained
here not because it separated the generators in this benchmark which was
nearly flat (\S\ref{sec:results:rq1:empirical}) but as a safeguard
whose near-zero value certifies the absence of direct training-set
memorisation. A compact suite that omitted it could not distinguish a
genuinely low-leakage generator from one that was simply never audited for
it. In medical applications where interpretable
effect preservation is central, Cox confidence-interval overlap should be
added to this minimal suite. Where model interpretability is central,
feature-importance agreement should also be retained.

\subsection*{Answer to RQ1}
\label{sec:results:rq1:answer}

RQ1 is answered by a two-pillar evaluation framework rather than by a
single metric. Utility must be evaluated through both statistical
similarity and downstream predictive performance. Privacy must not only be evaluated
limited to a single metric. It must be
evaluated through proximity, inference, and outlier-specific disclosure
risk. The suitable metrics are those that jointly cover marginal fidelity,
joint structure, classifier transferability, clinically interpretable
association preservation, record proximity, membership leakage, attribute
leakage, and tail-record linkability. Aggregate views are useful for
communicating the resulting privacy-utility trade-offs, but they remain
interpretive summaries rather than replacements for the raw metric values.
For future compact evaluations, a reduced subset of these instruments can be
recommended for a first-pass screening, provided it retains at least one
metric on each utility and privacy axis identified above. The specific
composition is specified in Section~\ref{sec:results:rq1:minimal-suite}.

\subsubsection{Statistical Similarity}
\label{sec:results:rq2:utility:similarity}

Statistical similarity is reported along the three complementary views
defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:similarity}: per-column marginal

evaluated on the aligned feature space introduced in

\paragraph{Per-Column Marginal Distributions:}
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2:marginals} overlays the real and synthetic marginals
for a representative block of columns spanning ordinal field
(\emph{age}), binary outcome (\emph{readmitted}), categorical codes
(\emph{admission\_\allowbreak type\_\allowbreak id}, \emph{A1Cresult}), and continuous clinical
counts (\emph{time\_\allowbreak in\_\allowbreak hospital},
\emph{num\_\allowbreak lab\_\allowbreak procedures},
\emph{num\_\allowbreak medications},
\emph{number\_\allowbreak inpatient}). Four of the five
generators, namely HealthGAN and the three Pythia variants, reproduce the
real marginals closely. Their bars and density curves overlie the real
reference across the categorical and continuous columns alike, with only a
mild tendency to over-concentrate mass on the dominant category of highly
skewed fields such as \emph{A1Cresult}. PATE-GAN is the consistent
exception. It flattens the \emph{readmitted} distribution towards an even
split rather than the real 66:34 ratio, assigns substantial probability
mass to \emph{age} and \emph{admission\_\allowbreak type\_\allowbreak id} categories that are
rare or absent in the real data. It also inverts the \emph{A1Cresult}
distribution by placing most of its mass on minority categories instead of
the dominant one. On the continuous fields it displaces
\emph{time\_\allowbreak in\_\allowbreak hospital},
\emph{num\_\allowbreak lab\_\allowbreak procedures}, and
\emph{num\_\allowbreak medications} markedly to the right of their real
supports. The
remaining column blocks follow the same qualitative split, and the full
per-column gallery for all five generators is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:distributions}. Together, Mean KS and PCD summarise two
basic characteristics of statistical fidelity: whether individual columns

\subsubsection{Predictive Performance}
\label{sec:results:rq2:utility:predictive}

Predictive utility is measured under the Train-on-Synthetic,
Test-on-Real (TSTR) protocol defined in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive:tstr}: a classifier is trained
on each synthetic table and evaluated on the held-out real test split
($n = 14{,}304$) with \emph{readmitted} as the binary target. The
Train-on-Real, Test-on-Real (TRTR) model provides the upper-bound
reference. Three downstream classifiers are reported
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive:classifiers}). Gradient
Boosting is taken as the headline model, with Logistic Regression and
Random Forest included.

\paragraph{Class-Balance Diagnostic:}
Before interpreting the threshold-dependent classifier metrics, the
positive-class prevalence of the binary readmission target was checked
across real and synthetic train/test splits. The real train and test splits
both have a readmission rate of approximately $0.340$. PATE-GAN
substantially over-produces the positive class in both synthetic splits
($0.498$ train, $0.507$ test), whereas HealthGAN remains close to the real
prevalence and the Pythia variants match the real split proportions by
construction through class-conditioned generation. This diagnostic is not a
predictive-utility metric, but it helps interpret F1, precision, and recall,
because target-prevalence shifts can change classifier operating behaviour
independently of feature quality. The full class-balance table and
TRTR/TSTR/TRTS/TSTS AUC-ROC matrix are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:class-balance}.

\paragraph{TSTR Classifier Performance:}
Table~\ref{tab:rq2:tstr} reports the full Train-Source $\times$ Classifier
matrix under TSTR, with the Train-on-Real baseline included for reference.
Ranked by the threshold-independent AUC-ROC, the headline Gradient-Boosting
column orders the generators Pythia-1B $(0.6818)$, Pythia-70M $(0.6634)$,
HealthGAN $(0.6312)$, Pythia-1B-DP $(0.5827)$, and PATE-GAN $(0.5108)$,
against the real reference of $0.7008$. Pythia-1B therefore recovers
approximately $97\%$ of the real-data AUC-ROC, whereas PATE-GAN performs
close to the chance level. The threshold-dependent F1 score shows a
different pattern: HealthGAN and the two non-private Pythia models exceed
the real-trained baseline F1 of $0.3668$, whereas Pythia-1B-DP falls below
it at $0.2714$. The two non-private Pythia models reach approximately
$0.50$. This does not indicate stronger discrimination,
since their AUC-ROC remains below the real reference. It instead reflects an
operating-point shift, in which the synthetic-trained models predict the
minority (readmitted) class more frequently. Recall approximately doubles,
from $0.26$ for the real-trained model to between $0.51$ and $0.53$ for the
non-private Pythia models, at the cost of precision, which falls from
$0.63$ to between $0.47$ and $0.50$. AUC-ROC and F1 therefore capture
different facets of predictive utility, consistent with the coverage
summary of Table~\ref{tab:rq1-coverage}. PATE-GAN performs poorly on both:
its Gradient-Boosting F1 of $0.0093$ corresponds to a classifier that
rarely predicts the positive class (recall $0.0047$).

\paragraph{Cox Proportional-Hazards Agreement:}
Beyond classification, agreement on a Cox proportional-hazards model
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox}) tests whether the
synthetic data preserve survival-style association patterns. A Cox model is
fitted on each source, and agreement is summarised by the fraction of
covariates whose synthetic $95\%$ hazard-ratio confidence interval overlaps
the corresponding real interval
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox-overlap}). The overlap
summary in Table~\ref{tab:rq2:cox} is used as the main result because it is
the scalar Cox metric defined in the methodology; the full per-covariate
hazard-ratio plots are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:cox}.

HealthGAN and the two 1B-parameter Pythia variants show the strongest
agreement on this metric, with seven overlapping covariates each
(HealthGAN: $7/12$; Pythia-1B and Pythia-1B-DP: $7/13$). Pythia-70M
overlaps on four of thirteen covariates, while PATE-GAN overlaps on none.
The identical overlap counts for Pythia-1B and Pythia-1B-DP indicate that
the utility loss observed under TSTR AUC-ROC is not mirrored by this
coarser Cox-overlap diagnostic. This should be read as agreement in fitted
Cox effect-size intervals, not as evidence that the synthetic data validate
clinical causal relationships.

\subsubsection{Ablation: Age Binning Granularity}
\label{sec:results:rq2:utility:age-binning}

The preprocessing pipeline (\S\ref{sec:data:fe:age}) retains the dataset's
native ten-decade encoding of \emph{age} after an earlier design that
collapsed it into three coarse groups (young / middle-aged / elderly) was
abandoned. Table~\ref{tab:age-binning-tstr} quantifies the impact on
Train-on-Synthetic, Test-on-Real (TSTR) utility for the three generators
with matched runs under both age encodings: PATE-GAN, HealthGAN, and
Pythia-70M. The ablation is therefore a preprocessing-sensitivity check,
not a full five-generator comparison. Pythia-1B and Pythia-1B-DP are not
included because they were introduced after the preprocessing pipeline had
already been revised to the retained ten-bin age encoding; no matched

\subsection{Privacy Results}
\label{sec:results:rq2:privacy}

Privacy is assessed through the empirical instruments defined in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:privacy}: distance-based proximity metrics,
including the outlier-linkability check, and inference-attack simulations.
These diagnostics measure specific disclosure signals rather than
providing a formal privacy guarantee. For distance-based interpretation,
the reference scale is the real data's own spacing: the median
real-to-real nearest-neighbour distance, computed in the standardised
feature space, is $2.019$. Synthetic records at comparable or larger
distances are therefore less concerning on the proximity axis, but these
distances must be read alongside the utility results because off-manifold
synthetic data can also appear artificially private. Selected privacy
diagnostics are summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:rq2:privacy}.

\subsubsection{Nearest-Neighbour Distance and Distance Ratio}
\label{sec:results:rq2:privacy:nn}

Table~\ref{tab:rq2:nn} reports, for each generator, the median and
fifth-percentile real-to-synthetic and synthetic-to-real nearest-neighbour
distances together with the nearest-neighbour distance ratio (NNDR) defined
in Sections~\ref{sec:eval:privacy:nn} and~\ref{sec:eval:privacy:nndr}. The
fifth percentile is the more privacy-relevant summary, since it isolates
the tail of synthetic records lying closest to real records, whereas the
median reflects typical records of little disclosure risk. An
NNDR near or above unity indicates that, on the median distance scale,
synthetic records are not closer to real records than real records are to
one another. This is a proximity-risk diagnostic, not standalone evidence
of privacy.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Median and fifth-percentile nearest-neighbour distances, with the
median distance ratio, per generator. The fifth-percentile synthetic-to-real
distance is the disclosure-relevant tail, that is, the closest synthetic
records to the real data. The real-to-real median nearest-neighbour distance
is $2.019$ for reference. Larger synthetic-to-real distances and
$\text{NNDR} \geq 1$ are consistent with lower median proximity risk only
when the generator also remains close to the real-data support.}
\label{tab:rq2:nn}
\input{images/results/tables/3a-nn_distances}
\end{table}

The four non-collapsed generators produce synthetic records whose median
distances to real data are of the same order as the real intrinsic
spacing. HealthGAN attains an NNDR of $1.063$, just above unity, so its
median synthetic-to-real distance is slightly larger than the real-to-real
reference scale. The three Pythia variants sit below unity (NNDR
$0.698$-$0.763$), indicating mildly closer median proximity to real
records than the real data show to themselves. This should be interpreted
as a proximity signal rather than evidence of record copying: their median
synthetic-to-real distances remain non-zero ($1.406$-$1.533$ in the
standardised feature space), although they are below the real-to-real
median. PATE-GAN is an outlier on this metric, with a synthetic-to-real
median distance of $219.879$ and an NNDR of $104.601$. Given its
similarity failure in Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2:utility:similarity},
these large distances are better interpreted as off-manifold drift than as
useful privacy protection. This illustrates the blind spot of
distance-based privacy metrics noted in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-coverage}: a
generator that fails to reproduce the real-data support can appear highly
private on proximity metrics alone.

The fifth-percentile distances confirm that the median comparison is not
concealing a close-proximity tail. For the four faithful generators the
fifth-percentile synthetic-to-real distances lie between $0.727$ and
$1.136$ in the standardised feature space, well above zero, so even the
closest synthetic records do not coincide with real records; this is
consistent with the absence of training-set memorisation reported in
Sections~\ref{sec:results:rq2:privacy:nnaa}
and~\ref{sec:results:rq2:privacy:mia}. PATE-GAN's fifth-percentile
synthetic-to-real distance ($79.995$) is inflated by the same off-manifold
drift as its median and does not indicate protection.

\subsubsection{Nearest-Neighbour Adversarial Accuracy}
\label{sec:results:rq2:privacy:nnaa}

The nearest-neighbour adversarial accuracy (NNAA;
Section~\ref{sec:eval:privacy:nnaa}) decomposes into a train-set and a
test-set adversarial accuracy, whose difference, the privacy loss,
measures memorisation: a value near zero indicates that the generator
treats training and held-out records indistinguishably and has therefore
not overfit to its training split. Table~\ref{tab:rq2:nnaa} reports both
components.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Nearest-neighbour adversarial accuracy per generator. Train and

\label{tab:rq2:nnaa}
\input{images/results/tables/3a_ii-nnaa}
\end{table}

The privacy loss is negligible for every generator (between $-0.0001$ and
$+0.0076$), so there is no meaningful evidence that any generator

None reaches the ideal of $0.5$, so all remain statistically
distinguishable from real data, but the near-zero privacy loss supports
the interpretation that this distinguishability does not arise from
training-set memorisation.

\subsubsection{Pareto Analysis}
\label{sec:results:rq2:pareto}

To answer the second half of RQ2, which family achieves a more effective
balance, this subsection applies the Pareto-dominance rule defined for the
privacy-utility landscape in Section~\ref{sec:eval:aggregate:tradeoff}.
No separate Pareto chart is introduced; the analysis is computed on the
same raw two-axis plane shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rq2:landscape}, using
Gradient-Boosting TSTR AUC-ROC as the utility axis and median NNDR as the
privacy axis. A generator is Pareto-optimal if no other generator is at
least as good on both axes and strictly better on one.

On the full set, the Pareto frontier consists of PATE-GAN, HealthGAN,
Pythia-70M, and Pythia-1B, and Pythia-1B-DP is the only dominated
generator, because HealthGAN attains both higher utility ($0.631$ vs.\
$0.583$) and a higher NNDR ($1.063$ vs.\ $0.763$). PATE-GAN appears on the frontier only because its
off-manifold output produces an extreme NNDR value while its utility is
lowest. This point is therefore retained for landscape completeness but is
not treated as a usable privacy-utility solution. Excluding PATE-GAN, the
usable frontier consists of HealthGAN, Pythia-70M, and Pythia-1B. The
two non-private LLMs occupy the high-utility end, while HealthGAN occupies
the higher-NNDR end. This frontier count should be read as a description
of the evaluated model set, not as a family-level dominance claim.

The dominated point is also informative. Pythia-1B-DP is dominated on this
AUC-ROC/NNDR plane because the utility reduction relative to Pythia-1B (a
$0.099$ AUC drop) is not accompanied by an NNDR increase large enough to
move it above HealthGAN. This does not mean that differential privacy is
ineffective in general. Membership-inference risk is near baseline across
the faithful generators, and attribute-inference signals are small except
for the mild primary-diagnosis signal previously observed for Pythia-1B. A
distance-only privacy axis therefore cannot represent the
attribute-leakage reduction achieved by Pythia-1B-DP
(Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2:privacy:aia}). This limitation of the
selected two-axis plane, together with the within-family question of what
differential privacy costs, motivates the dedicated DP-toggle analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:results:rq3}.

\subsection*{Answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results:rq2:answer}

The GAN family spans the two extremes of the evaluated landscape.
HealthGAN is the strongest non-private GAN operating point in this
comparison: it attains the best marginal fidelity (mean KS $0.0480$), the
best survival agreement (Cox overlap $0.583$), and moderate predictive
utility (AUC $0.631$ against the real ceiling of $0.701$), with membership
and attribute leakage near the random-guess baseline. Its median distance
ratio is the only one among the usable generators that sits at the NNDR
unity reference ($1.063$); this is read as neither memorising nor drifting
away from the real data rather than as a privacy advantage in itself,
because a ratio above unity can equally reflect distributional drift
(\S\ref{sec:eval:privacy:nndr}). PATE-GAN, the differentially private GAN
evaluated here, sits at the opposite extreme: at $\varepsilon = 5.0$ it
performs poorly on both utility branches (mean KS $0.5381$, AUC $0.511$,
Cox overlap $0$ of $13$ covariates). Its very large distance-based privacy
values are therefore interpreted as a consequence of off-manifold output
rather than as evidence of a usable privacy-utility solution. Among the
GAN generators evaluated, one offers high usable utility without a formal
guarantee (HealthGAN) and the other a formal guarantee without usable
utility (PATE-GAN), but not both in the same generator.

The LLM family is more uniform and occupies the higher-utility region of
the plane. Pythia-1B is the closest of all generators to the real-data
utility ceiling (AUC $0.682$ against $0.701$) and also achieves the best
joint-dependency fidelity (correlation difference $0.0206$) and the best
predictive F1; Pythia-70M trails it only slightly (AUC $0.663$) despite
its far smaller scale. Both show near-baseline membership-inference risk
and, apart from Pythia-1B's mild primary-diagnosis signal, limited
attribute-inference risk under the selected attacks. The differentially
private Pythia-1B-DP retains meaningful utility under a formal guarantee
(AUC $0.583$, above chance) and removes the small attribute-leakage signal
observed for its non-private counterpart, at the cost of a $0.099$ drop in
predictive utility. The LLM family therefore provides the two
highest-utility non-private points and the only differentially private
generator that remains usable.

Taken together, the answer to RQ2 has two parts. First, across the usable
generators the privacy axis barely separates the models:
membership-inference advantage and NNAA privacy loss remain near baseline,
while NNAA adversarial accuracy indicates that all synthetic outputs remain
distinguishable from the real data to some degree. The only structured
privacy signal is Pythia-1B's mild primary-diagnosis leakage. The
privacy-utility comparison on this dataset is therefore decided mainly on
utility retention rather than on a wide spread of disclosure risk. Second,
under this utility-led reading
neither family dominates the plane outright, and the two occupy different
regions under different requirements. Where no formal guarantee is needed,
both offer useful operating points: the LLM generators for peak
statistical and predictive utility (Pythia-1B, Pythia-70M), and HealthGAN
for strong marginal fidelity with a distance ratio at the NNDR unity
reference. The choice between them is a trade-off rather than a clear win.

Once a formal differential privacy guarantee at $\varepsilon = 5.0$ is
required, the evidence in this study favours the language-model approach:
the differentially private LLM (Pythia-1B-DP) preserves usable utility and
removes the observed attribute-leakage signal, whereas the differentially
private GAN (PATE-GAN) collapses to near-chance utility. This contrast is
between two specific generators rather than a general property of the two
families, and it is not a like-for-like mechanism comparison: the
differentially private LLM is a pretrained model fine-tuned under DP-SGD,
whereas PATE-GAN is trained from scratch, so part of the language-model
advantage under differential privacy reflects pretraining rather than the
base architecture alone. With that scope, the more effective balance under
a formal guarantee at the tested budget is achieved by the differentially
private language model, a conclusion examined directly through the
within-family DP-toggle analysis of Section~\ref{sec:results:rq3}.

\section{RQ3 - The Cost of DP Enforcement, Within-Family}
\label{sec:results:rq3}

% RQ3 (finalised): "At a matched privacy budget (ε=5.0), how does enforcing
% differential privacy using mechanisms appropriate to each family affect
% utility, and how do the resulting privacy-utility profiles differ
% between GAN-based (PATE-GAN vs HealthGAN) and LLM-based (Pythia 1B-DP vs
% Pythia 1B) synthesisers of tabular medical data?"
%
% The four generators analysed in this section are a subset of the five
% reported in \S\ref{sec:results:rq2}; the same experimental outputs are
% re-examined under a within-family DP-toggle lens, not re-run. Pythia-70M
% is excluded from RQ3's main comparison because DP-SGD failed at that
% scale (\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:pythia-70m-failure}).

\subsection{Convergence Failure of DP-SGD at the Pythia-70M Scale}
\label{sec:results:rq3:pythia-70m-failure}

An initial attempt to apply DP-SGD to Pythia-70M at $\varepsilon = 5.0$
failed to train usefully: the recorded training loss worsened across the
five epochs (from $3.38$ to $3.74$, peaking at $3.79$) under the
calibrated noise multiplier $\sigma = 0.436$, and the model did not
converge to a useful synthesis distribution. This behaviour is consistent
with the injected noise dominating the useful gradient signal at this
scale, although gradient magnitudes were not instrumented directly. The LLM-side DP comparison was therefore scaled to
Pythia-1B, which trained successfully under DP-SGD at the same privacy
budget. This itself constitutes a finding: DP-SGD on small LLMs is
impractical at moderate privacy budgets, consistent with prior work
showing that larger LLMs tolerate DP noise more
gracefully~\cite{Li2022LargeDP}. The methodological consequence, that the
LLM-family DP toggle is operationalised at the 1B scale, is discussed in
\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp:why-1b}.

\subsection{Cost of Differential Privacy on the GAN Family}
\label{sec:results:rq3:gan-toggle}

The GAN-family comparison contrasts the non-private HealthGAN with the
differentially private PATE-GAN. Because PATE-GAN incorporates differential
privacy by construction whereas HealthGAN does not, the contrast measures
the cost of moving to a formally private generator within the GAN family.
Table~\ref{tab:rq3:gan-toggle} reports the change on each headline metric.

\centering
\small

non-private value; on a utility row a negative $\Delta$ denotes a loss.}
\label{tab:rq3:gan-toggle}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Metric & HealthGAN & PATE-GAN & $\Delta$ \\
\midrule
Mean KS $\downarrow$       & 0.0480 & 0.5381 & $+0.4901$ \\

The transition is destructive across the entire utility pillar. Marginal
fidelity collapses (mean KS rises more than tenfold, $0.0480 \to 0.5381$),
joint-dependency fidelity worsens by a factor of roughly three
(correlation difference $0.0336 \to 0.1142$), and the predictive branch
collapses: AUC-ROC falls by $0.1204$ to near chance, F1
drops from $0.3824$ to $0.0093$, and the survival model loses every
confidence-interval overlap it previously held ($7/12 \to 0/13$). The
apparent privacy improvement, namely NNDR rising from $1.063$ to $104.601$,
is not a genuine gain but the off-manifold artefact established in
Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2:privacy:nn}; the simulation-based privacy
measures barely move, since both generators already resist membership and
attribute inference. Crucially, this comparison is not a clean
differential-privacy toggle: HealthGAN and PATE-GAN are architecturally
distinct generators, so the measured cost conflates the DP mechanism with
the architecture change, and the utility collapse cannot be attributed to
differential privacy alone
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:limit:gan-toggle-asymmetry}).

\subsection{Cost of Differential Privacy on the LLM Family}
\label{sec:results:rq3:llm-toggle}

The LLM-family comparison contrasts the non-private Pythia-1B with the
differentially private Pythia-1B-DP. Because both variants share the same
Pythia-1B backbone, the same row-as-text serialisation, and the same
low-rank fine-tuning configuration, this comparison is a clean
differential-privacy toggle: the only material difference is the DP-SGD
training procedure. The private
variant was trained under a privacy budget that the accounting reports as
an achieved $\varepsilon = 4.998$ against a target of $5.0$ (with
$\delta = 10^{-5}$, gradient-clipping norm $1.0$, noise multiplier
$0.789$, and an effective batch size of $512$).

The cost is real and falls almost entirely on the predictive branch, where
it is uneven: AUC-ROC drops by a moderate $0.0991$ (to $0.5827$, still well

$+0.0410 \to -0.0056$), and outlier linkability falls to zero. Differential
privacy on the LLM family therefore trades a predictive-utility reduction,
moderate on AUC-ROC but larger on F1, for a measurable reduction in
attribute disclosure, while preserving distributional fidelity and
survival-model agreement.

\subsection*{Answer to RQ3}
\label{sec:results:rq3:answer}

At a matched privacy budget of $\varepsilon = 5.0$, the two families reach
markedly different privacy-utility endpoints under differential privacy.
The LLM-family toggle (Pythia-1B $\to$ Pythia-1B-DP) loses $0.0991$ in TSTR
AUC-ROC while leaving statistical similarity essentially intact (mean KS
$+0.0187$) and survival-model agreement unchanged ($7/13$ confidence-interval
overlaps before and after), and it converts this moderate utility cost into
a real privacy gain: the observed attribute-inference leak is removed
(maximum advantage $+0.0410 \to -0.0056$) and outlier linkability falls to
zero. The GAN-family toggle (HealthGAN $\to$ PATE-GAN) instead loses
$0.1204$ in AUC-ROC and collapses the rest of the utility pillar (mean KS
$+0.4901$, Cox overlap $7/12 \to 0/13$), while its only apparent privacy
gain is the off-manifold distance artefact rather than a genuine reduction
in disclosure. This cross-family difference must, however, be read with the
asymmetry caveat: the LLM toggle isolates the DP-SGD effect on a shared
backbone, whereas the GAN toggle conflates the differential-privacy
mechanism with an architecture change between two distinct generators
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:limit:gan-toggle-asymmetry}). Even under that
caveat, the practical conclusion is consistent across every reported
metric: at $\varepsilon = 5.0$
the LLM family yields a differentially private generator that remains in
the usable region of the privacy-utility plane, whereas the GAN family's
differentially private instance does not.
