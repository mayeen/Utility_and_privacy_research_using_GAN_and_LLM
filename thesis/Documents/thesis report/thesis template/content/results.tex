\subsection{Operationalising the Two-Pillar Framework}
\label{sec:results:rq1:operationalising}

The evaluation framework adopted to answer RQ1 is the two-pillar
structure of \emph{utility} (statistical similarity to the real data and
predictive performance of downstream models) and \emph{privacy}
(instance-level distance metrics and inference-attack simulations), as
operationalised on the Diabetes 130-US Hospitals benchmark across eleven
distinct single-number metrics. The full per-metric definitions, the data-preparation
steps that precede them, and the experimental parameters under which
they are computed are not restated here; they are given in
Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation}. This subsection therefore serves
only as an orientation: it names the two pillars and their three
branches (similarity, predictive performance, privacy), and it forward
references the methodology chapter for every operational detail.
The distinct data-characteristic dimension that each metric is designed
to capture is the substantive part of the RQ1 answer and is treated
per-metric in Section~\ref{sec:results:rq1:diagnostic}.

\subsection{Distinct Data-Characteristic Coverage of the Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:results:rq1:diagnostic}

The headline numerical summary of the evaluation framework
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:aggregate:table}) reports eleven single-number
metrics per generator, organised across the three branches defined in
the methodology chapter: statistical similarity, predictive performance,
and privacy. Each of these metrics is designed to capture a distinct
dimension of data characteristics, and the branches are intended to be
jointly necessary rather than individually sufficient. The
design-intent dimension and primary blind spot of every metric in this
suite are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-coverage}; the statements
below are restated in compact form from
Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation}, to which the reader is referred for
the full operational definitions, the data preparation that precedes
each metric, and the precise parameters under which it is computed.

\label{tab:rq1-coverage}

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two members of the privacy branch (membership-inference AUC and
membership-inference advantage) report the same underlying adversary
score on two scales --- AUC and AUC minus the random-guess baseline of
$0.5$ respectively. Both are listed because the advantage form is the
quantity directly thresholdable against the privacy-risk bands defined
in Section~\ref{sec:eval:privacy:mia}, whereas the AUC form aligns
with the convention adopted elsewhere in the membership-inference
literature~\cite{Shokri2017MIA}; reporting both makes the same finding
readable under either framing without re-deriving the conversion. No
other metric pair in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-coverage} expresses the same
underlying quantity in two forms.

The branches as a whole are designed to be jointly necessary rather
than individually sufficient. A generator can score well on the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic and fail on the pairwise correlation
difference if it preserves marginal shape but distorts joint linear
dependency; it can score well on the pairwise correlation difference
and fail on TSTR AUC-ROC if the linear-correlation structure of the
synthetic table is faithful but the conditional distribution that
supports a downstream classifier is shifted; and it can score well on
TSTR AUC-ROC and fail on the privacy axis if the conditional structure
that makes a classifier learn the readmission target also encodes
enough record-level detail to support membership inference. The
empirical positions of the five generators evaluated in this thesis on
each of these metric axes are reported per pillar in
Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2}, and combined into the cross-family
summary table of Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2:table}.

\subsection*{Answer to RQ1}
\label{sec:results:rq1:answer}

RQ1 is answered along its two halves. Surveying the synthetic-data
evaluation literature in Section~\ref{sec:bg:eval} and operationalising
it for this thesis in Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation} yields a
two-pillar framework in which utility decomposes into two complementary
branches --- statistical similarity to the real data and predictive
performance of downstream models --- and privacy is reported separately
through instance-level distance metrics and explicit inference-attack
simulations. Within this framework, the eleven single-number metrics
enumerated in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-coverage} constitute the set of
metrics deemed suitable for evaluating the privacy and utility of
synthetic tabular medical data in the configuration adopted here. Each
of these metrics is designed to capture a distinct dimension of data
characteristics --- worst-case marginal discrepancy, joint linear
dependency, threshold-independent transferability of conditional
structure under TSTR, balance of false positives and false negatives at
the default decision threshold, agreement of clinically interpretable
effect sizes, bulk-level point proximity, scale-normalised proximity,
aggregate training-set leakage on two reporting scales, worst-case
conditional attribute leakage, and outlier-specific disclosure risk
--- and the suite as a whole covers these dimensions across the three
branches without redundancy within any single branch beyond the
deliberate two-scale reporting of membership inference. The
recommended minimal subset of these eleven metrics that still covers
both pillars is examined separately in
Section~\ref{sec:results:rq1:minimal-suite}.

\subsubsection{Statistical Similarity}
\label{sec:results:rq2:utility:similarity}

Statistical similarity is reported along the three complementary views
defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:similarity}: per-column marginal
agreement, pairwise linear-dependency agreement, and a global
manifold-overlap check. All five generators and the real reference are
evaluated on the aligned feature space introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:data:fe}.

\paragraph{Per-Column Marginal Distributions.}
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2:marginals} overlays the real and synthetic marginals
for a representative block of columns spanning categorical
(\texttt{gender}, \texttt{admission\_type\_id}), ordinal (\texttt{age}),
an institutional disposition code (\texttt{discharge\_disposition\_id}),
and continuous clinical counts (\texttt{num\_lab\_procedures},
\texttt{num\_medications}). Four of the five generators --- HealthGAN and
all three Pythia variants --- reproduce the real marginals closely: their
bars and density curves overlie the real reference across categorical and
continuous columns alike. PATE-GAN is the consistent exception. It
assigns probability mass to category codes that do not occur in the real
data (for example a spurious third \texttt{gender} level), flattens the
sharply peaked \texttt{discharge\_disposition\_id} distribution into a
broad smear, and displaces continuous fields such as
\texttt{num\_medications} and \texttt{num\_lab\_procedures} markedly to
the right of their real supports. The remaining column blocks follow the
same qualitative split; the full per-column gallery for all five
generators is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:distributions}.

\subsubsection{Predictive Performance}
\label{sec:results:rq2:utility:predictive}

Predictive utility is measured under the Train-on-Synthetic,
Test-on-Real (TSTR) protocol defined in
Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive:tstr}: a classifier is trained
on each synthetic table and evaluated on the held-out real test split
($n = 14{,}304$) with \texttt{readmitted} as the binary target. The
Train-on-Real, Test-on-Real (TRTR) model provides the upper-bound
reference. Three downstream classifiers are reported
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive:classifiers}); Gradient
Boosting is taken as the headline model, with Logistic Regression and
Random Forest included for robustness.

\paragraph{Class-Balance Diagnostic.}
Before interpreting the threshold-dependent classifier metrics, the
positive-class prevalence of the binary readmission target was checked
across real and synthetic train/test splits. The real train and test splits
both have a readmission rate of approximately $0.340$. PATE-GAN
substantially over-produces the positive class in both synthetic splits
($0.498$ train, $0.507$ test), whereas HealthGAN remains close to the real
prevalence and the Pythia variants match the real split proportions by
construction through class-conditioned generation. This diagnostic is not a
predictive-utility metric, but it helps interpret F1, precision, and recall,
because target-prevalence shifts can change classifier operating behaviour
independently of feature quality. The full class-balance table and
TRTR/TSTR/TRTS/TSTS AUC-ROC matrix are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:class-balance}.

\paragraph{TSTR Classifier Performance.}
Table~\ref{tab:rq2:tstr} reports the full Train-Source $\times$ Classifier
matrix. Read by threshold-independent ranking (AUC-ROC), the headline
Gradient-Boosting column orders the generators
Pythia-1B $(0.6818) >$ Pythia-70M $(0.6634) >$ HealthGAN $(0.6312) >$
Pythia-1B-DP $(0.5827) >$ PATE-GAN $(0.5108)$, against the real ceiling of
$0.7008$. Pythia-1B thus recovers roughly $97\%$ of the real-data
discriminative signal, while PATE-GAN sits almost on the chance diagonal.
A notable inversion appears on the threshold-dependent F1 score: the three
Pythia variants and HealthGAN all exceed the real-trained baseline's
F1 ($0.3668$), with both non-private Pythia models reaching
$\approx 0.50$. This does not indicate better discrimination --- their AUC
remains below the real ceiling --- but reflects an operating-point shift:
the synthetic-trained models predict the minority (readmitted) class far
more liberally, roughly doubling recall ($0.51$--$0.53$ vs.\ the real
model's $0.26$) at the cost of precision ($0.47$--$0.50$ vs.\ $0.63$). The
two metrics therefore capture different facets of predictive utility, as
anticipated by the coverage map of Table~\ref{tab:rq1-coverage}. PATE-GAN
collapses on both: its Gradient-Boosting F1 of $0.0093$ corresponds to a
classifier that almost never predicts the positive class
(recall $0.0047$).

\paragraph{Survival-Model Agreement.}
Beyond classification, agreement on a Cox proportional-hazards model
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox}) tests whether the
synthetic data preserve clinically interpretable effect sizes. A Cox model
is fitted on each source, and agreement is summarised by the fraction of
covariates whose synthetic $95\%$ hazard-ratio confidence interval
overlaps the corresponding real interval
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox-overlap}); the per-generator
fractions are reported in Table~\ref{tab:rq2:cox}. HealthGAN and the two
1B-parameter Pythia variants recover the largest share of real hazard

one, including sign-level disagreements such as the \texttt{change}
covariate (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2:cox}, left). Notably, the non-private and
differentially private Pythia-1B variants agree on the same number of
covariates (seven of thirteen), so the differential-privacy cost that is
clearly visible in the TSTR discrimination scores is not apparent in the
coarser Cox-overlap measure.

\subsubsection{Ablation: Age Binning Granularity}
\label{sec:results:rq2:utility:age-binning}

The preprocessing pipeline (\S\ref{sec:data:fe:age}) retains the dataset's
native ten-decade encoding of \texttt{age} after an earlier design that
collapsed it into three coarse groups (young / middle-aged / elderly) was
abandoned. Table~\ref{tab:age-binning-tstr} quantifies the impact on
Train-on-Synthetic, Test-on-Real (TSTR) utility for all three non-DP
generators (PATE-GAN, HealthGAN, Pythia-70M), evaluated on the real
held-out test split ($n = 14{,}304$) with \texttt{readmitted} as the
binary target. Gradient Boosting is reported as the headline downstream
classifier; results for Logistic Regression and Random Forest follow the
same qualitative pattern.
\vspace{0.5em}

Two observations frame the analysis. First, the Train-on-Real upper bound
is essentially invariant between the two encodings (TRTR AUC-ROC: $0.7008$
under ten-bin vs.\ $0.7014$ under three-bin), confirming that any

synthesis family.

\paragraph{Pythia-70M: large gain under finer binning.}
The token-based LLM generator benefits unambiguously from the ten-decade
encoding: AUC-ROC rises by $+0.0653$ ($0.5981 \rightarrow 0.6634$) and
F1 by $+0.1061$ ($0.3944 \rightarrow 0.5005$), with the same
direction observed under Logistic Regression
($\Delta\text{AUC} = +0.0442$, $\Delta\text{F1} = +0.0992$) and Random
Forest ($\Delta\text{AUC} = +0.0671$, $\Delta\text{F1} = +0.1123$).
Because each field is emitted as a discrete token, the three-bin scheme
collapsed the entire $0$--$100$ year range into three vocabulary items,
erasing conditional structure between age and clinically correlated
features (\texttt{number\_diagnoses}, \texttt{number\_inpatient},
\texttt{insulin}). The ten-decade encoding restores a finer conditioning
vocabulary and recovers age-specific co-occurrence patterns relevant to
readmission prediction.

\paragraph{HealthGAN: metric-dependent direction.}
HealthGAN exhibits the opposite ranking behaviour to Pythia: AUC-ROC is
slightly \emph{higher} under three-bin ($0.6605$ vs.\ $0.6312$,
$\Delta = -0.0293$ moving to ten-bin). The threshold-based F1, however,
moves in the reverse direction, $0.2647 \rightarrow 0.3824$
($+0.1177$ in favour of ten-bin), driven primarily by recall recovery
($0.1697 \rightarrow 0.2984$). The interpretation is that HealthGAN did
not capture the fine-grained ten-bin age structure: under the coarser
encoding its distributional output is closer to the simpler target and
yields marginally better probability ranking, but the synthetic data
over-concentrates on the majority (non-readmit) class and suppresses
positive-class recall. The ten-bin encoding therefore degrades AUC
marginally while restoring usable clinical decision performance.

\paragraph{PATE-GAN: differential-privacy noise dominates.}
PATE-GAN remains close to chance under both encodings (AUC-ROC $0.4823$
three-bin vs.\ $0.5108$ ten-bin); the binning choice produces only a
$+0.0285$ AUC shift, an order of magnitude smaller than the Pythia
delta. F1 is near zero in either case ($0.0437$ vs.\ $0.0093$). The
DP-PATE noise mechanism dominates over the encoding-level signal, so the
generator does not meaningfully exploit either age representation. This
sets the floor against which the within-family DP-utility cost is later
quantified in \S\ref{sec:results:rq3:gan-toggle}.
\vspace{0.5em}

Taken together, the three generators react to the same preprocessing
change in three distinct regimes: a token-based LLM that gains
substantially from finer categorical cardinality, a continuous-latent GAN
whose ranking and threshold behaviour diverge under the change, and a
DP-noised GAN that is largely insensitive to it. This generator-specific
encoding sensitivity is discussed further in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion:gan-vs-llm}.

\subsection{Privacy Results}
\label{sec:results:rq2:privacy}

Privacy is assessed through the two complementary classes of instrument
defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval:privacy}: instance-level distance
metrics, which ask how close synthetic records lie to real records, and
inference-attack simulations, which ask whether an adversary can recover
membership or attribute information from the synthetic data. Throughout,
the natural scale of reference is the spacing of the real data itself: the
median real-to-real nearest-neighbour distance is $2.019$, so a synthetic
record at a comparable or larger distance carries no proximity-based
disclosure beyond what the real distribution already exhibits. The
simulation-based views are consolidated in Figure~\ref{fig:rq2:privacy}.

the blind spot of distance-based privacy metrics noted in
Table~\ref{tab:rq1-coverage}: a generator that fails utility entirely can
appear maximally private, so PATE-GAN's distances must be read jointly
with its similarity failure rather than as genuine protection.

\subsection{Pareto Analysis}
\label{sec:results:rq2:pareto}

To answer the second half of RQ2 --- which family achieves a more
effective balance --- the generators are assessed for Pareto optimality on
the (utility, privacy) pair used in the landscape of
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2:landscape}, taking predictive utility as the
Gradient-Boosting TSTR AUC-ROC and privacy as the median NNDR. A generator
is Pareto-optimal if no other generator is at least as good on both axes
and strictly better on one.

On the full set, the Pareto frontier is
$\{\text{PATE-GAN}, \text{HealthGAN}, \text{Pythia-70M}, \text{Pythia-1B}\}$,
and Pythia-1B-DP is the only dominated generator --- HealthGAN attains both
a higher utility ($0.631$ vs.\ $0.583$) and a higher NNDR ($1.066$ vs.\
$0.771$). PATE-GAN appears on the frontier only by occupying the
degenerate maximum-privacy, minimum-utility corner, where its position is
the distance artefact discussed above rather than a genuinely attractive
operating point. Setting that degenerate corner aside, the usable frontier
is $\{\text{HealthGAN}, \text{Pythia-70M}, \text{Pythia-1B}\}$: the two
non-private LLMs anchor the high-utility end, while HealthGAN anchors the
high-privacy (NNDR) end. The LLM family therefore contributes two of the
three usable Pareto-optimal points and the GAN family one.

The single dominated generator is instructive. Pythia-1B-DP is dominated
not because differential privacy is intrinsically costly on this plane, but
because its utility cost (a $0.099$ AUC drop from Pythia-1B) is not repaid
by any NNDR-measurable privacy gain: membership and attribute leakage are
already negligible for every faithful generator, so the distance-based
privacy axis cannot reward the DP variant for the attribute-leakage
reduction it does achieve (Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2:privacy:aia}). This
limitation of a single composite plane --- and the within-family question
of what differential privacy actually costs --- motivates the dedicated
DP-toggle analysis of Section~\ref{sec:results:rq3}.

\subsection*{Answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results:rq2:answer}

The GAN family spans the two extremes of the landscape. HealthGAN is the
strongest single operating point produced by any generator without a
formal guarantee: it attains the best marginal fidelity (mean KS $0.0480$)
and the best survival agreement (Cox overlap $0.583$), respectable
predictive utility (AUC $0.631$), and is the only usable generator whose
distance ratio clears the NNDR privacy threshold ($1.066$), with
membership and attribute leakage indistinguishable from chance. PATE-GAN,
the GAN family's differentially private member, sits at the opposite
extreme: at $\varepsilon = 5.0$ it collapses on both utility branches
(mean KS $0.5381$, AUC $0.511$, Cox overlap $0/13$), and its apparently
perfect privacy scores are the artefact of off-manifold output rather than
genuine protection. The GAN family therefore offers either high usable
utility without a formal guarantee (HealthGAN) or a formal guarantee
without usable utility (PATE-GAN), but not both.

The LLM family is more uniform and occupies the high-utility region of the
plane. Pythia-1B is the closest of all generators to the real-data utility
ceiling (AUC $0.682$ against $0.701$) and additionally achieves the best
joint-dependency fidelity (correlation difference $0.0206$) and the best
predictive F1; Pythia-70M trails it only slightly (AUC $0.663$) despite
its far smaller scale. Both resist membership inference and, apart from
Pythia-1B's mild leakage on the primary diagnosis, attribute inference as
well. The differentially private Pythia-1B-DP retains meaningful utility
under a formal guarantee (AUC $0.583$, well above chance) and removes the
one attribute-leakage signal its non-private counterpart exhibited, at the
cost of a $0.099$ drop in predictive utility. The LLM family thus provides
the two highest-utility non-private points and the only differentially
private generator that remains usable.

Taken together, the answer to RQ2 is that neither family dominates the
privacy--utility plane outright, but they dominate different regions and
under different requirements. Where no formal guarantee is needed, both
families offer strong operating points --- the LLM family for peak
statistical and predictive utility (Pythia-1B, Pythia-70M), and the GAN
family for the best fidelity combined with a distance ratio that clears
the privacy threshold (HealthGAN) --- and the choice between them is a
genuine trade-off rather than a clear win. Once a formal differential
privacy guarantee at $\varepsilon = 5.0$ is required, however, the balance
tips decisively toward the LLM family: its DP member preserves usable
utility while its GAN counterpart does not. The more effective balance
under a formal guarantee is therefore achieved by the LLM family, a
conclusion examined directly through the within-family DP-toggle lens of
Section~\ref{sec:results:rq3}.

\section{RQ3 -- The Cost of DP Enforcement, Within-Family}
\label{sec:results:rq3}

% RQ3 (finalised): "At a matched privacy budget (ε=5.0), how does enforcing
% differential privacy using mechanisms appropriate to each family affect
% utility, and how do the resulting privacy--utility profiles differ
% between GAN-based (PATE-GAN vs HealthGAN) and LLM-based (Pythia 1B-DP vs
% Pythia 1B) synthesisers of tabular medical data?"
%
% The four generators analysed in this section are a subset of the five
% reported in \S\ref{sec:results:rq2}; the same experimental outputs are
% re-examined under a within-family DP-toggle lens, not re-run. Pythia-70M
% is excluded from RQ3's main comparison because DP-SGD failed at that
% scale (\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:pythia-70m-failure}).

\subsection{Convergence Failure of DP-SGD at the Pythia-70M Scale}
\label{sec:results:rq3:pythia-70m-failure}

An initial attempt to apply DP-SGD to Pythia-70M at $\varepsilon = 5.0$
failed to train usefully: the injected noise exceeded the gradient
magnitudes, and the model did not converge to a useful synthesis
distribution. The LLM-side DP comparison was therefore scaled to
Pythia-1B, which trained successfully under DP-SGD at the same privacy
budget. This itself constitutes a finding: DP-SGD on small LLMs is
impractical at moderate privacy budgets, consistent with prior work
showing that larger LLMs tolerate DP noise more
gracefully~\cite{Li2022LargeDP}. The methodological consequence ---
that the LLM-family DP toggle is operationalised at the 1B scale --- is
discussed in \S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp:why-1b}.

\subsection{Cost of Differential Privacy on the GAN Family}
\label{sec:results:rq3:gan-toggle}

The GAN-family comparison contrasts the non-private HealthGAN with the
differentially private PATE-GAN.

Because PATE-GAN incorporates differential privacy by construction whereas
HealthGAN does not, the contrast measures the cost of moving to a formally
private generator within the GAN family.
Table~\ref{tab:rq3:gan-toggle} reports the change on each headline metric.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Within-family differential-privacy transition for the GAN family
(HealthGAN $\to$ PATE-GAN). $\Delta$ is the private value minus the
non-private value; on a utility row a negative $\Delta$ denotes a loss.}
\label{tab:rq3:gan-toggle}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Metric & HealthGAN & PATE-GAN & $\Delta$ \\
\midrule
Mean KS $\downarrow$       & 0.0480 & 0.5381 & $+0.4901$ \\
Corr.\ diff.\ $\downarrow$ & 0.0336 & 0.1142 & $+0.0806$ \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The transition is destructive across the entire utility pillar. Marginal
fidelity collapses (mean KS rises more than tenfold, $0.0480 \to 0.5381$),
joint-dependency fidelity worsens by a factor of roughly three
(correlation difference $0.0336 \to 0.1142$), and the predictive branch is
effectively wiped out: AUC-ROC falls by $0.1204$ to near chance, F1
drops from $0.3824$ to $0.0093$, and the survival model loses every
confidence-interval overlap it previously held ($7/12 \to 0/13$). The
apparent privacy improvement --- NNDR rising from $1.066$ to $105.082$ ---
is not a genuine gain but the off-manifold artefact established in
Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2:privacy:nn}; the simulation-based privacy
measures barely move, since both generators already resist membership and
attribute inference. Crucially, this comparison is not a clean
differential-privacy toggle: HealthGAN and PATE-GAN are architecturally
distinct generators, so the measured cost conflates the DP mechanism with
the architecture change, and the utility collapse cannot be attributed to
differential privacy alone
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:limit:gan-toggle-asymmetry}).

\subsection{Cost of Differential Privacy on the LLM Family}
\label{sec:results:rq3:llm-toggle}

The LLM-family comparison contrasts the non-private Pythia-1B with the
differentially private Pythia-1B-DP.

Because both variants share the same Pythia-1B backbone, the same
row-as-text serialisation, and the same low-rank fine-tuning
configuration, this comparison is a clean differential-privacy toggle: the
only material difference is the DP-SGD training procedure. The private
variant was trained under a privacy budget that the accounting reports as
an achieved $\varepsilon = 4.998$ against a target of $5.0$ (with
$\delta = 10^{-5}$, gradient-clipping norm $1.0$, noise multiplier
$0.789$, and an effective batch size of $512$).
Table~\ref{tab:rq3:llm-toggle} reports the change on each headline metric.

The cost is real but contained, and it falls almost entirely on the
predictive branch: AUC-ROC drops by $0.0991$ (to $0.5827$, still well

is barely affected --- mean KS rises by only $0.0187$ and the correlation

confidence-interval overlap ($7/13$) before and after. On the privacy side
the toggle produces a genuine, if small, benefit: the attribute-inference
advantage on the primary diagnosis, the one structured leak exhibited by
the non-private model, is removed (maximum advantage
$+0.0410 \to -0.0056$), and outlier linkability falls to zero. Differential
privacy on the LLM family therefore trades a moderate predictive-utility
reduction for a measurable reduction in attribute disclosure, while
preserving distributional fidelity and survival-model agreement.

The two arrows differ sharply in length and meaning. The LLM arrow is

\subsection*{Answer to RQ3}
\label{sec:results:rq3:answer}

At a matched privacy budget of $\varepsilon = 5.0$, the cost of enforcing
differential privacy differs sharply between the two families, and the LLM
family's privacy--utility profile degrades far more gracefully. The
LLM-family toggle (Pythia-1B $\to$ Pythia-1B-DP) loses $0.0991$ in TSTR
AUC-ROC while leaving statistical similarity essentially intact (mean KS
$+0.0187$) and survival-model agreement unchanged ($7/13$ confidence-interval
overlaps before and after), and it converts this moderate utility cost into
a real privacy gain --- the sole attribute-inference leak is removed
(maximum advantage $+0.0410 \to -0.0056$) and outlier linkability falls to
zero. The GAN-family toggle (HealthGAN $\to$ PATE-GAN) instead loses
$0.1204$ in AUC-ROC and collapses the rest of the utility pillar (mean KS
$+0.4901$, Cox overlap $7/12 \to 0/13$), while its only apparent privacy
gain is the off-manifold distance artefact rather than a genuine reduction
in disclosure. This cross-family difference must, however, be read with the
asymmetry caveat: the LLM toggle isolates the DP-SGD effect on a shared
backbone, whereas the GAN toggle conflates the differential-privacy
mechanism with an architecture change between two distinct generators
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:limit:gan-toggle-asymmetry}). Even under that
caveat, the practical conclusion is unambiguous: at $\varepsilon = 5.0$
the LLM family yields a differentially private generator that remains in
the usable region of the privacy--utility plane, whereas the GAN family's
differentially private instance does not.
