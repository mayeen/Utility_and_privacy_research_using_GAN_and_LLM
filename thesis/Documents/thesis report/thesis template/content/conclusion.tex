\section{Overall Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion:overall}

This thesis shows that synthetic medical data cannot be evaluated through a
single utility or privacy score. Statistical similarity, downstream
predictive performance, record proximity, inference attacks, and outlier
linkability capture different properties and must be interpreted together.
In the present benchmark, utility metrics separated the evaluated
configurations more clearly than the selected privacy attacks. The
non-private Pythia configurations produced the strongest predictive results
and preserved pairwise linear relationships well, while HealthGAN provided
the strongest marginal similarity. These results identify different
strengths rather than one generator family that is superior for every use.

The private configurations also produced different observed outcomes at the
same target privacy parameters. Pythia-1B-DP retained substantially more
measured utility than the evaluated PATE-GAN configuration, although its
predictive performance was lower than that of Pythia-1B. PATE-GAN produced
data far from the real-data distribution and did not provide a usable
privacy-utility balance under the selected evaluation. However, this
comparison does not establish that DP-SGD is generally superior to PATE,
because the configurations differ in model family, architecture, and privacy
mechanism. Taken together, the findings support multidimensional evaluation
and configuration-specific model selection under clearly stated privacy,
utility, and governance requirements.

\section{Contributions and Practical Implications}
\label{sec:conclusion:contributions}

% Migrated and lightly edited from proposal §7 with thesis-specific scope
% (five generators rather than three).
This thesis delivers a systematic and rigorously controlled comparison of
GAN-based and LLM-based methods for privacy-preserving synthetic medical data
generation. By evaluating HealthGAN, PATE-GAN, Pythia-70M, Pythia-1B, and
Pythia-1B-DP under a unified experimental framework on the
\emph{Diabetes 130-US Hospitals} dataset~\cite{diabetes_db}, the study
establishes a transparent basis for analysing the privacy-utility trade-off
across fundamentally different generative paradigms. The integration of aligned 
training conditions, consistent preprocessing, and a twelve-metric evaluation 
pipeline ensures comparability across models. It helps ensure that differences 
in performance reflect model characteristics rather than experimental variability.

The four concrete contributions of this work are the following.

\paragraph{Unified evaluation pipeline:} A reproducible two-pillar framework 
is used. It comprises \emph{utility}, split into statistical similarity to 
the real data and predictive performance of downstream models, and \emph{privacy}. 
The framework is instantiated as twelve single-number metrics applied identically to
every generator under comparison.

\paragraph{Head-to-head benchmark:} The study directly compares 
five generators across model family, privacy
setting, and LLM scale: HealthGAN~\cite{healthgan},
PATE-GAN~\cite{pategan}, Pythia-70M-LoRA and
Pythia-1B-LoRA~\cite{Biderman2023Pythia,Hu2022LoRA,Miletic2024}, and
Pythia-1B-DP-LoRA trained with Opacus
DP-SGD~\cite{Yousefpour2021Opacus,Abadi2016DPSGD}.
The Pythia-1B non-DP scale anchor and the Pythia-1B-DP variant together 
constitute a scope extension beyond the originally submitted proposal. 
They yield the first within-family DP-cost comparison for tabular LLM 
synthesis on this benchmark at an architecturally controlled scale.

\paragraph{Changes under the private configurations:} Quantitative evidence
is provided on the changes observed under a target privacy setting of
$(\varepsilon,\delta)=(5.0,10^{-5})$. The Pythia comparison holds the
backbone scale constant and measures the change from Pythia-1B to
Pythia-1B-DP. The GAN comparison reports the transition from HealthGAN to
PATE-GAN. Because these GAN configurations use different architectures and
training procedures, their difference is not interpreted as the marginal
effect of differential privacy alone.

\paragraph{Practical guidance:} A decision-oriented summary explains how the
evaluated configurations differ when predictive performance, statistical
similarity, empirical privacy risk, or a formal privacy requirement is given
priority. Among the two private configurations, Pythia-1B-DP retained the
stronger measured utility on this benchmark. This is a configuration-specific
finding and is not evidence that DP-SGD is generally preferable to PATE.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion:future}

% Concrete extensions the thesis does not pursue but flags as natural
% follow-ups, each tied to a finding or limitation of the present study.
The findings reported above suggest several concrete extensions that this
thesis does not pursue but that follow naturally from its scope and
limitations.

\subsection{Extended Evaluation Across Datasets and Privacy Budgets}
\label{sec:conclusion:future:robustness}
\label{sec:conclusion:future:cross-data}
\label{sec:conclusion:future:epsilon}

All results are obtained on a single benchmark~\cite{diabetes_db}, so the
generator rankings cannot be assumed to transfer to other clinical settings.
Repeating the unified pipeline on richer EHR corpora such as MIMIC-IV~\cite{mimic} 
or eICU would test the generality of these findings. These datasets contain 
more continuous variables, higher attribute cardinality, and explicit temporal 
structure. Such experiments would test whether the observed utility patterns
and the poor result of the evaluated PATE-GAN configuration persist in other
clinical settings. Repeated runs with different random seeds would also allow
uncertainty around the recorded rankings to be estimated.

The privacy comparison is conducted at a 
single matched budget of $(\varepsilon = 5.0, 
\delta = 10^{-5})$. This gives one point on each 
configuration's privacy-utility curve rather than its slope. Repeating PATE-GAN and
Pythia-1B-DP across a range of budgets, such as $\varepsilon \in \{1, 3, 5, 10\}$,
would test whether the PATE-GAN result improves at looser budgets and how the
utility of Pythia-1B-DP changes as the privacy requirement is tightened.

\subsection{Privacy and Evaluation Improvements}
\label{sec:conclusion:future:privacy-evaluation}
\label{sec:conclusion:future:adversaries}
\label{sec:conclusion:future:nonlinear}

The privacy pillar relies on a distance-based membership-inference adversary
and a predictor-based attribute-inference attack. Both are comparatively weak
threat models, so the negligible leakage they report
(Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2:privacy}) is a lower bound on disclosure risk
rather than a guarantee. Future work should evaluate stronger adversaries, 
including shadow-model membership inference~\cite{Shokri2017MIA}, training-data 
extraction~\cite{Carlini2021Extracting}, and white-box attacks. These attacks 
would test that bound. By comparing the differentially private variants against 
their non-private counterparts, such work could show whether the formal guarantee 
delivers protection that the present attacks are too weak to detect.

The statistical-similarity branch quantifies joint structure through the linear
correlation difference. Extending it with a non-linear measure such as a
mutual-information matrix difference~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval} would capture
dependencies that linear correlation cannot.

Query-specific differentially private data generation could be investigated,
where only the columns required for a particular analysis are synthesized. 
Restricting generation to fewer attributes may reduce dimensionality and 
concentrate the privacy budget on the most relevant marginals, potentially 
improving data utility. Building on MST and Private-PGM, these marginals 
could be selected dynamically according to each analytical query. Further 
evaluation is needed to examine the trade-off between improved query 
accuracy and reduced general-purpose usability\cite{McKenna2021NIST}.

\subsection{Larger and More Complex Data-Generation Settings}
\label{sec:conclusion:future:larger-complex}
\label{sec:conclusion:future:llm}
\label{sec:conclusion:future:longitudinal}

Pythia-1B produced the strongest predictive results among the evaluated
generators but still trailed the real-data ceiling. The Pythia-70M DP-SGD
run was not carried forward for evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:results:rq3:pythia-70m-failure}). These observations motivate
testing larger autoregressive models under the same low-rank adaptation
strategy. Prior studies report improved synthetic-data utility with larger
Pythia models~\cite{Miletic2024} and better private fine-tuning performance
from larger pretrained language models~\cite{Li2022LargeDP}. Larger backbones
may therefore improve the observed privacy-utility profile, although their
per-sample-gradient computation would require substantially more memory and
processing time.

This study treats each encounter as one independent cross-sectional row.
Extending the comparison to time-series synthesis
\cite{dash2020medical,bhanot2022investigating} and to multimodal records
combining tabular fields with imaging or free-text notes would test whether the
observed findings persist when temporal and cross-modal dependencies must also
be preserved.

\section{Closing Remarks}
\label{sec:conclusion:closing}

% Migrated and lightly adjusted from proposal §7 closing paragraphs.
This thesis combines utility assessment with instance-level privacy assessment. 
Utility spans both distributional similarity to the real data and predictive 
performance of downstream models. This combined evaluation determines not only 
which models perform best, but also under which conditions each class of models 
is most appropriate. This enables a nuanced interpretation of when non-DP 
synthesis is sufficient. It also clarifies when formal differential privacy 
is necessary. Finally, it shows how LLM-based generators behave in terms of 
memorisation and disclosure risk in clinical settings. Beyond generating 
comparative insights, the thesis contributes a
reusable, methodologically grounded evaluation protocol that can support
future research and practical governance. Although the study focuses on
tabular EHR-like data and a defined set of generative models, the framework
provides a foundation for subsequent extensions to longitudinal records,
multimodal data, and emerging DP-enhanced LLMs. Ultimately, the findings are
intended to inform model selection, guide responsible data-release strategies,
and strengthen the methodological basis for safe and high-utility synthetic
data use in healthcare research.
