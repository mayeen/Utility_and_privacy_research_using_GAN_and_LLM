\section{Medical Tabular Data and Privacy Regulations}
\label{sec:bg:regulations}

Electronic health records are typically stored as high-dimensional tables that
combine categorical, ordinal, and continuous fields and contain both directly
and indirectly identifying attributes. Therefore, it makes their secondary use valuable
for research yet sensitive from a privacy standpoint~\cite{Murtaza2023}. Because
the sharing of such records is constrained by data-protection regulations such
as the GDPR and HIPAA (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}), the conventional response
has been to de-identify the data before release through classical anonymisation.

These classical techniques include generalisation, suppression, distortion,
swapping, and masking, together with privacy models such as $k$-anonymity and
$\ell$-diversity~\cite{anonymization,park2013perturbed}. On high dimensional
health data, however, they struggle to preserve analytical utility and have
repeatedly been shown vulnerable to linkage and inference
attacks~\cite{anonymization,Murtaza2023}. Such metrics were 
devised for the suppression-and-generalisation
setting and cannot be applied directly to model-based
synthesisers~\cite{park2013perturbed}.
Synthetic data generation sidesteps the direct release of real records, but it
does not automatically resolve the privacy-utility trade-off. Synthetic 
data produced by generative models without explicit privacy
protection does not shield outlier records from linkage attacks since an
adversary with access to the synthetic dataset can infer the presence of a
target record with high confidence~\cite{stadler2022synthetic}. This motivates
the formal privacy machinery introduced in Section~\ref{sec:bg:dp} and adopted
by the differentially private generators evaluated in this thesis.

\section{Synthetic Data Generation: A Taxonomy}
\label{sec:bg:taxonomy}

Synthetic tabular data generators fall broadly into three families. Statistical
models include copulas, Bayesian networks, and sequential
trees~\cite{shi2025comprehensive, emam2021optimizing}. 
Deep generative models are GAN and VAE-based.
Language-model-based approaches instead treat rows as
text~\cite{shi2025comprehensive,Borisov2023GReaT}.
Alongside these data-driven families, a knowledge-driven approach builds
generators from hand-curated expert rules and a hybrid approach blends the
two~\cite{Murtaza2023}. Both rely on hand-crafted domain knowledge and therefore fall
outside the fully data-driven scope of this thesis. Each
family admits a differentially private variant, yielding the two axes along which
the five generators evaluated in this thesis are positioned. The generative
paradigm contrasts generative adversarial networks with large language
models. The privacy regime contrasts non-private training with
differential privacy. The
remainder of the chapter develops the two families used here
(§\ref{sec:bg:gan}, §\ref{sec:bg:llm}) and the DP machinery they rely on
(§\ref{sec:bg:dp}). Compared with image and text data, tabular data combines categorical and
numerical variables with different statistical properties. This
heterogeneity makes generation difficult and has encouraged the
development of several distinct approaches~\cite{shi2025comprehensive}.
Within each family, known failure modes (training instability and mode collapse in GANs,
a tendency toward over-averaged outputs in VAEs, and hallucination in LLMs) have
motivated successive specialisations, such as the diffusion-model and
fine-tuned-LLM branches reviewed in the same survey~\cite{shi2025comprehensive}.


\section{GAN-based Approaches for Tabular Data}
\label{sec:bg:gan}

GANs constitute the dominant deep generative
paradigm for tabular synthesis, yet exhibit the training instability and
mode-collapse pathologies~\cite{shi2025comprehensive}. The remainder of this section reviews
the architectural progression from the original minimax formulation through
the Wasserstein and gradient-penalty refinements that stabilise adversarial
training (§\ref{sec:bg:gan:foundations}). It then examines the two
healthcare-oriented variants situated on the DP and non-DP axes evaluated
in this thesis: HealthGAN (§\ref{sec:bg:gan:health}) and
PATE-GAN (§\ref{sec:bg:gan:pategan}).


\subsection{Foundations: GAN, WGAN, WGAN-GP}
\label{sec:bg:gan:foundations}

Generative adversarial networks formulate data generation as a
minimax game between a generator $G$, which maps noise samples into
the data space, and a discriminator $D$, which estimates the
probability that a given sample originates from the data distribution
rather than from generator~\cite{Goodfellow2014GAN}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gan-minimax}
\min_{G} \max_{D}\; V(D, G) =
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[\log D(x)\right]
+ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\!\left[\log\bigl(1 - D(G(z))\bigr)\right].
\end{equation}
The global optimum is attained when the generator distribution
matches the data distribution and the discriminator is everywhere
indifferent. In practice, however, optimisation of this objective is
fragile because the discriminator can saturate and starve the generator of
gradient signal. GAN training is difficult to monitor because the value 
function does not provide
a steadily improving signal of convergence. The generator may also suffer from
mode collapse, where it produces samples from only a limited part of the real
data distribution instead of capturing its full diversity.

These problems are linked to the implicit Jensen--Shannon divergence in
the original GAN objective. This divergence can become discontinuous with
respect to the generator parameters when the real and generated
distributions have disjoint or nearly disjoint
supports~\cite{Arjovsky2017WGAN}. Replacing it with the Wasserstein-1
distance restores continuity throughout training and yields a
gradient that remains informative even in this regime. Via the
Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, the Wasserstein-1 distance admits
the variational form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wasserstein-dual}
W(p_{\text{data}}, p_g)
= \sup_{\|f\|_{L} \leq 1}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[f(x)\right]
- \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_g}\!\left[f(x)\right],
\end{equation}
so that the discriminator is replaced by a 1-Lipschitz \emph{critic}
whose scalar output approximates this supremum. The Lipschitz
constraint is enforced in the original Wasserstein GAN by clipping
the critic's weights to a fixed interval. This modification
produces markedly stabler training and a loss that correlates with
sample quality.

Weight clipping was shown to be the source of
its own problem, chief among them a sharp loss of critic
capacity and gradient norms that either explode or vanish depending
on the clipping threshold~\cite{Gulrajani2017WGANGP}. The improved
formulation replaces clipping with a soft penalty on the critic's
input-gradient norm,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wgan-gp}
\mathcal{L}
= \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x} \sim p_g}\!\left[D(\tilde{x})\right]
- \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[D(x)\right]
+ \lambda \,
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x} \sim \mathbb{P}_{\hat{x}}}\!\left[
\bigl(\|\nabla_{\hat{x}} D(\hat{x})\|_{2} - 1\bigr)^{2}
\right],
\end{equation}
where $\hat{x}$ is sampled uniformly along straight lines between
paired real and generated samples. The penalty encourages the
critic to satisfy the Lipschitz condition with unit norm along the
region of the input space where the two distributions are being
compared, rather than uniformly across its parameters. This
Wasserstein-with-gradient-penalty formulation is the backbone on
which the HealthGAN generator evaluated in this thesis is built
(Section~\ref{sec:methods:healthgan}).

\subsection{Healthcare-specific Variants: HealthGAN}
\label{sec:bg:gan:health}

HealthGAN was introduced as a response to the limitations of medGAN. 
MedGAN was primarily limited to binary diagnosis codes and was shown to
introduce disease co-occurrences not found in the real data, limiting
the resemblance and utility of its synthetic output. These limitations made medGAN
unsuitable for mixed-type EHR data~\cite{healthgan}.
To address
these shortcomings, the model combines medGAN's design with the WGAN-GP
framework. It uses the Wasserstein distance and a tailored data
transformation pipeline to support continuous and categorical variables. Its architecture
employs large batch sizes to ensure that outliers and rare clinical values are
reliably captured, and incorporates a bottleneck to reduce memorisation and
maintain a compact model footprint. This facilitates secure model export outside
the training environment~\cite{healthgan}.

A key contribution of HealthGAN is the introduction
of Nearest-Neighbour Adversarial Accuracy (NNAA), a metric suite that
enables joint assessment of resemblance and privacy and has been recognised in
systematic reviews in synthetic health data
evaluation~\cite{Murtaza2023}. Empirical results show that HealthGAN was the
only model in a six-method benchmark that maintained privacy while supporting
model export, achieving competitive utility and resemblance relative to
established baselines~\cite{healthgan}.

\subsection{PATE-GAN}
\label{sec:bg:gan:pategan}

\textsc{PATE-GAN} applies the Private Aggregation of Teacher Ensembles
(PATE) framework to generative modelling. It trains multiple teacher
discriminators on separate data partitions and combines their predictions
using a differentially private noisy aggregation mechanism. So it provides formal
$(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP guarantees while avoiding direct exposure of private
gradients~\cite{pategan,Papernot2017PATE}. This provides a tunable privacy
budget with an explicit accountant. Reviews of synthetic 
health data evaluation emphasise that privacy
should be assessed explicitly rather than assumed, including through
differential-privacy-based mechanisms and empirical disclosure or inference
metrics~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023}. 
PATE-GAN offers a principled middle ground with controllable 
$\varepsilon$ in domains where non-DP
synthetic data has been shown to leave outliers
vulnerable~\cite{stadler2022synthetic}. It also offers the same benefit  
where DP marginal-based approaches (e.g., PrivSyn~\cite{privsyn2021}) 
trade utility for formal privacy guarantees.

\section{LLM-based Approaches for Tabular Data}
\label{sec:bg:llm}

Large language models offer a generative paradigm that is structurally
distinct from the adversarial approach of \S\ref{sec:bg:gan}. A decoder-only 
transformer does not learn an implicit sampler through a minimax game. Instead, 
it models the joint distribution of a record explicitly as a product of autoregressive
conditionals over a tokenised representation of that record. This formulation
confers two properties that are attractive for
heterogeneous clinical tables. First, mixed-type columns are handled without
the mode-specific normalisation or one-hot expansion required by conditional
tabular GANs. Because every value is
rendered as text whether numeric or categorical~\cite{Miletic2024}. 
Second, the self-attention mechanism
models dependencies across all columns natively, which mitigates the
difficulty GANs encounter as dimensionality grows~\cite{Miletic2024}.
The remainder of this section describes the row-as-text serialisation that
makes tabular data amenable to language modelling
(\S\ref{sec:bg:llm:tabula}), the Pythia model family used as the synthesiser
(\S\ref{sec:bg:llm:pythia}), the low-rank adaptation scheme used for
parameter-efficient fine-tuning (\S\ref{sec:bg:llm:lora}), and the
differentially private regime within which this thesis situates the
privacy-preserving variant of its LLM synthesiser (\S\ref{sec:bg:llm:dp}).

\subsection{Tabula and the Row-as-Text Paradigm}
\label{sec:bg:llm:tabula}

The serialisation underlying language-model-based tabular synthesis was
introduced by the GReaT framework, which encodes each record as a
natural-language sentence~\cite{Borisov2023GReaT}. A row with features
$f_1, \dots, f_m$ and corresponding values $v_1, \dots, v_m$ is rendered as a
sequence of clauses of the form ``$f_i$ is $v_i$'', concatenated into a single
token sequence $s = (w_1, \dots, w_T)$. During training a random permutation
of the feature order is applied to each row, so that the model is not biased
towards a fixed column ordering and can subsequently be conditioned on any
subset of features at sampling time~\cite{Borisov2023GReaT}. The language
model is then fit with the standard autoregressive objective, which
factorises the sequence probability as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:llm:ar}
p_\theta(s) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta\!\left(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}\right),
\end{equation}
and is trained by minimising the token-level negative log-likelihood
$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(w_t \mid w_{<t})$. New
records are generated by sampling from this distribution and parsing the
emitted text back into typed feature values.

Tabula extends GReaT with modifications that target the efficiency
limitations of applying full natural-language models to tabular
data~\cite{Zhao2023Tabula}. It compresses the token representation of feature
names and categorical values to shorten sequences. It also applies a middle padding
strategy, and considers initialising the network from random weights rather
than from a corpus-pretrained checkpoint. In the process it removes the need for the model
to unlearn natural-language priors that are irrelevant to tabular
structure~\cite{Zhao2023Tabula}. Building on this framework, the 
approach serialises rows with token-sequence
compression and left padding, and conditions generation on the binary target
variable as the leading token~\cite{Miletic2024}. The Pythia models are then
benchmarked against CTGAN, a conditional tabular GAN that employs mode-specific
normalisation~\cite{Xu2019CTGAN}. 
They use a train-on-synthetic, test-on-real (TSTR) protocol with a random-forest 
estimator. They report that the language models match or surpass the GAN 
baseline, with utility increasing in model size. The comparison between 
randomly initialised and pretrained starting points was inconclusive. That study evaluates
utility alone and reports no privacy assessment, a gap that motivates the
present work.

\subsection{The Pythia Model Family}
\label{sec:bg:llm:pythia}

Pythia is a suite of decoder-only autoregressive transformers released to
support controlled study of how language models behave across scale and
training~\cite{Biderman2023Pythia}. The models span parameter counts from
70 million to twelve billion, and share a common architecture and a
single training corpus presented in an identical data order. It was done so that
differences in behaviour can be attributed to scale rather than to confounded
data or optimisation choices~\cite{Biderman2023Pythia}. All checkpoints and
intermediate training states are publicly released, which makes the suite a
reproducible basis for fine-tuning experiments.

This thesis uses two members of the suite as scale anchors. Pythia-70M serves
as a compute-tractable baseline whose training and sampling costs are modest.
Pythia-1B is the largest configuration examined in the benchmark 
study and the scale at which the utility advantage of
language-model synthesis over tabular GANs is most pronounced~\cite{Miletic2024}. 
Although it's important to note that in some dataset it is reported that 
the gain is negligible. In our case, restricting the
comparison to these two sizes isolates the effect of model capacity on the
privacy-utility trade-off while keeping the experimental budget feasible.

\subsection{LoRA Fine-tuning for Parameter-Efficient Adaptation}
\label{sec:bg:llm:lora}

Fine-tuning every parameter of a billion-parameter model is costly in memory
and storage. It is especially burdensome under differentially private
optimisation, which requires per-example gradient computation~\cite{Yu2022DPFT}. Low-Rank
Adaptation (LoRA) reduces this cost by freezing the pretrained weight matrices
and learning a low-rank update in their place. For a
pretrained weight matrix $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, LoRA parameterises
the adapted layer as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:llm:lora}
h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + B A\, x,
\end{equation}
where $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$ and the
rank $r \ll \min(d, k)$. The matrix $A$ is initialised from a random Gaussian
distribution and $B$ is initialised to zero, so that $\Delta W = 0$ at the
start of training and the adapted model initially coincides with the
pretrained one. During training, $\Delta W x$ is scaled by
$1/r$ so that the magnitude of its coordinates stays roughly $\Theta(1)$ as the
rank $r$ varies, which reduces the need to retune the learning rate when $r$ is
changed~\cite{Hu2022LoRA}. Only $A$ and $B$ are updated, which reduces the
number of trainable parameters per layer from $dk$ to $r(d + k)$, typically by
several orders of magnitude.

This reduction is important for the differentially private fine-tuning
discussed in \S\ref{sec:bg:dp:dpsgd}. DP-SGD requires per-example 
gradients and adds Gaussian noise during
the update. So restricting optimisation to the low-dimensional LoRA parameters
reduces the number of trainable coordinates and lowers the memory overhead of
per-example gradient clipping. Parameter-efficient adapters attain privacy-utility
trade-offs competitive with, and in some settings superior to, full-model
private fine-tuning. That too at a small fraction of its trainable-parameter and memory
cost~\cite{Yu2022DPFT}. However, the evidence is mixed. With careful tuning,
full fine-tuning of large pretrained models remains a competitive differentially
private baseline that parameter-efficient methods do not necessarily
surpass~\cite{Li2022LargeDP}.

\subsection{DP-Augmented LLM Synthesis}
\label{sec:bg:llm:dp}

Language models are known to memorise and to
reproduce verbatim training records. This makes uncontrolled LLM synthesis a
privacy hazard rather than a safeguard~\cite{Carlini2021Extracting}. Two
routes to constraining this leakage appear in the literature. The first
perturbs the generation process directly. SafeSynthDP pairs LLM-driven
synthesis with calibrated Laplace and Gaussian noise and traces the resulting
downstream accuracy against the privacy parameter $\varepsilon$. It is reporting
competitive performance and claims resistance to membership inference on a
text-classification benchmark~\cite{Nahid2024}. The second
route enforces privacy during fine-tuning through DP-SGD
(\S\ref{sec:bg:dp:dpsgd}). Privately fine-tuning only 
parameter-efficient adapters can retain much of the utility of 
non-private language-model fine-tuning at single-digit $\varepsilon$ value~\cite{Yu2022DPFT}, 
and larger pretrained models retaining more of
their utility as the privacy constraint tightens~\cite{Li2022LargeDP}. This 
thesis adopts the second route where Pythia-1B with DP-SGD via Opacus is fine-tuned
to instantiate its differentially private variant. It then compares this model 
against the non-private Pythia variants and the GAN-based generators within a 
single privacy-utility evaluation.

\section{Differential Privacy}
\label{sec:bg:dp}

Two of the five generators carry formal differential
privacy guarantees. This section sets out the framework on which those
guarantees rest. Differential privacy limits how much any one individual 
record can affect the result of a randomized algorithm. This provides a 
strong worst-case privacy guarantee, even when an adversary has access 
to additional background information.~\cite{Dwork2014Foundations}. 
The remainder of this section introduces the $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ definition
together with the composition theorems that govern how privacy cost
accumulates over repeated computations (§\ref{sec:bg:dp:def}). The DP-SGD
algorithm and its Opacus implementation used to privately fine-tune the
language-model synthesiser (§\ref{sec:bg:dp:dpsgd}), the Private Aggregation
of Teacher Ensembles framework that underlies the privacy-preserving GAN
(§\ref{sec:bg:dp:pate}), and the Rényi differential privacy framework that
underpins the tight privacy accounting used for both differentially private
generators (§\ref{sec:bg:dp:rdp}).

\subsection{$(\varepsilon,\delta)$-Differential Privacy and Composition}
\label{sec:bg:dp:def}

A randomised mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ is said to satisfy
$(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy if, for every pair of neighbouring
datasets $D, D'$ that differ in a single record, and for every measurable
output subset $S \subseteq \mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{M})$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:dp:def}
\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] + \delta.
\end{equation}

The parameter $\varepsilon$ represents the privacy budget. Smaller values 
provide stronger privacy by placing tighter limits on how much the output 
distribution can change when one record is modified. When $\varepsilon = 0$, 
the outputs are statistically indistinguishable. The
relaxation term $\delta$ permits a small probability of privacy failure
and is conventionally required to be no larger, and preferably smaller, than the inverse dataset
size. This is because values at this threshold can be
satisfied by mechanisms that occasionally release complete
records~\cite{Dwork2014Foundations}.

The definition above compares the behaviour of a mechanism on neighbouring
datasets, so the amount of randomisation required depends on how much the
underlying query can change when one record is modified. This worst-case change
is the query's \emph{global sensitivity}. A deterministic query can therefore
be converted into a differentially private randomised mechanism by adding noise
calibrated to its sensitivity. The Laplace mechanism adds noise with scale
proportional to the sensitivity divided by $\varepsilon$ to satisfy pure
$\varepsilon$-DP, while the Gaussian mechanism adds noise calibrated to the
sensitivity, $\varepsilon$, and $\delta$ to satisfy approximate
$(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP~\cite{Dwork2006DP,Dwork2014Foundations}.

Iterative methods, such as the differentially private optimisation algorithms used in this
thesis, access the private dataset many times during training. Each noisy query consumes
part of the privacy budget, so composition rules are needed to measure how the total privacy
cost accumulates. Basic sequential composition states that $k$ mechanisms,
each $(\varepsilon, 0)$-differentially private, yield a
$(k\varepsilon, 0)$-private composite. But this linear bound is loose for
practical training regimes that invoke thousands of gradient computations.
The advanced composition theorem of Dwork and Roth tightens this linear
bound. For all $\varepsilon, \delta, \delta' \ge 0$, the class of
$(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differentially private mechanisms satisfies
$(\varepsilon', k\delta + \delta')$-differential privacy under $k$-fold
adaptive composition~\cite{Dwork2014Foundations}, with $\varepsilon'$ given by

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:dp:advcomp}
\varepsilon' = \sqrt{2k \ln(1/\delta')}\,\varepsilon + k\varepsilon(e^{\varepsilon} - 1).
\end{equation}

The leading term grows as $\sqrt{k}$, far more slowly than the linear
$k\varepsilon$ of basic composition, yet even this remains insufficient for
the repeated Gaussian noise injections of DP-SGD, motivating the tighter
accountants introduced in §\ref{sec:bg:dp:rdp}.

\subsection{DP-SGD and Opacus}
\label{sec:bg:dp:dpsgd}

Differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) modifies standard
mini-batch SGD by adding privacy protection to each training update. In ordinary
SGD, a batch of examples is used to estimate a gradient, and the model
parameters are moved in the direction that reduces the loss. In DP-SGD, this
gradient computation is made private by first limiting the contribution of each
individual training example and then adding calibrated Gaussian noise
before the parameter update~\cite{Abadi2016DPSGD}.

Let $\theta_t$ denote the model parameters at training step $t$, let
$\mathcal{L}$ be the loss function, let $C$ be the clipping threshold, let
$\sigma$ be the noise multiplier, and let $\eta_t$ be the learning rate. At each
step, a Poisson-sampled lot $\mathcal{B}_t$ of expected size $L$ is drawn from
the training data. For each selected example $x_i$, DP-SGD first computes the
per-example gradient:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:dp:perexample}
g_t(x_i) = \nabla_{\theta_t} \mathcal{L}(\theta_t, x_i).
\end{equation}

Each per-example gradient is then clipped so that its L2 norm is at most $C$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:dp:clip}
\bar{g}_t(x_i) \;\leftarrow\;
\frac{g_t(x_i)}
{\max\!\big(1,\; \lVert g_t(x_i) \rVert_2 / C\big)}.
\end{equation}

This clipping step ensures that no single record can contribute an arbitrarily
large update. If a gradient already has norm at most $C$, it is left unchanged.
Otherwise, it is rescaled to have norm $C$. The clipped per-example gradients 
are then summed, one Gaussian noise vector is
added to the sum, and the result is averaged to obtain the noisy gradient used
for the parameter update:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:dp:update}
\tilde{g}_t \;\leftarrow\; \frac{1}{L}\sum_{i}\!\big(\bar{g}_t(x_i)
   \;+\; \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma^{2} C^{2}\,\mathbf{I}\big)\big),
\qquad
\theta_{t+1} \;\leftarrow\; \theta_t - \eta_t\,\tilde{g}_t.
\end{equation}


The clipping threshold $C$ controls the maximum per-example contribution, while
the noise multiplier $\sigma$ controls the amount of Gaussian noise added after
clipping. For fixed sampling and training settings, a larger $\sigma$ gives a
stronger privacy guarantee but produces noisier gradients, which can reduce
model utility or slow convergence. Because DP-SGD accesses the private data
many times during training, the total privacy cost must be accumulated across
all training steps. This is done with a privacy accountant (§\ref{sec:bg:dp:rdp}) rather than by
naively adding the privacy loss of each update~\cite{Abadi2016DPSGD}.

The Opacus library provides a PyTorch implementation of DP-SGD
~\cite{Yousefpour2021Opacus}. It extends standard PyTorch training by adding
per-example gradient computation, gradient clipping, Gaussian noise injection,
Poisson sampling, and privacy accounting. In this thesis, Opacus is used to
train the differentially private Pythia variant. Consistent with the LoRA setup
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:bg:llm:lora}, the pretrained Pythia weights are
kept frozen and DP-SGD is applied only to the trainable low-rank adapter
parameters. Although the equation above shows the update in the form of
gradient descent, the implementation passes the clipped and noised gradient to
an Adam optimiser. The privacy-relevant operations remain the per-example
clipping, Gaussian noise addition, and privacy accounting. Method-specific
settings, including the chosen noise multiplier and target
$(\varepsilon,\delta)$, are reported in Section~\ref{sec:method:generators}.


\subsection{The PATE Framework}
\label{sec:bg:dp:pate}

The Private Aggregation of Teacher Ensembles (PATE) framework
offers an alternative route to
differentially private learning that avoids the explicit gradient perturbation
of DP-SGD~\cite{Papernot2017PATE}. The private training data is first 
partitioned into $N$ disjoint
subsets, and an independent \emph{teacher} model is trained on each partition
without any privacy mechanism. A separate \emph{student} model is then
trained on a corpus of unlabelled public data, with labels generated by
aggregating the teachers' predictions through a noisy voting mechanism:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:dp:pate}
f(x) \;=\; \arg\max_{j}\;\Big\{\, n_{j}(\vec{x}) \;+\; \mathrm{Lap}(1/\gamma)\,\Big\},
\end{equation}

where $n_j(\vec{x})$ denotes the number of teachers voting for class $j$ on
input $\vec{x}$ and $\mathrm{Lap}(1/\gamma)$ is Laplace noise with scale $1/\gamma$
controlling the privacy cost per query.

The partitioning step is central to the privacy argument. Since the private
records are split across disjoint teacher datasets, changing one individual's
record can affect only the teacher trained on that partition. For a given query,
this means that each class vote count can change by at most one and only the
noisy aggregate label is released rather than the individual teacher
predictions. Calibrating noise to these vote counts therefore gives a formal
$(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP guarantee for the labels returned by the aggregation
mechanism~\cite{Papernot2017PATE}. The student is then trained only on public
inputs paired with these noisy aggregate labels and never directly observes the
private training data. The privacy guarantee therefore carries over to the
trained student by the post-processing property of differential
privacy~\cite{Dwork2014Foundations}. PATE is more privacy-efficient 
when the teachers agree, because
high-consensus queries consume less privacy budget than queries with
divided votes. Later PATE variants replace Laplace voting noise
with Gaussian noise and analyse the resulting mechanism using Rényi
differential privacy~\cite{papernot2018scalable}
(§\ref{sec:bg:dp:rdp}). The use of PATE in generative adversarial networks is
described in §\ref{sec:bg:gan:pategan}.

\subsection{Rényi DP and the Moments Accountant}
\label{sec:bg:dp:rdp}

Private training repeatedly accesses sensitive data, so the privacy cost
must be accumulated across all training steps or aggregation queries.
Standard composition rules can produce loose bounds in settings such as
DP-SGD and PATE, motivating more precise accounting methods
\cite{Abadi2016DPSGD,Papernot2017PATE}.

Rényi Differential Privacy (RDP) is a relaxation of pure
$\varepsilon$-differential privacy based on Rényi divergence. The order
$\alpha>1$ determines how differences between the output distributions
of neighbouring datasets are measured. A randomised mechanism
$\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $(\alpha,\bar{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})$-RDP if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:dp:rdp}
D_{\alpha}\!\left(
  \mathcal{M}(D)\,\Vert\,\mathcal{M}(D')
\right)
\leq \bar{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}
\end{equation}
for every pair of neighbouring datasets $D$ and $D'$
\cite{Mironov2017RDP}.

RDP is particularly useful because privacy costs add under composition.
At a fixed order $\alpha$, mechanisms with costs
$\bar{\varepsilon}_{1,\alpha},\ldots,
\bar{\varepsilon}_{k,\alpha}$ have a total cost equal to their sum.
After composition, the RDP guarantee can be converted to
$(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP using
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bg:dp:conv}
\varepsilon_{\alpha}
=
\bar{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}
+
\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha-1}.
\end{equation}
When several orders are tracked, this conversion is evaluated at each
order and the smallest resulting $\varepsilon_{\alpha}$ is reported
\cite{Mironov2017RDP}.

RDP is closely related to the moments accountant, which tracks moments
of the privacy-loss random variable (PRV) across repeated private operations
\cite{Abadi2016DPSGD,Mironov2017RDP}. The PATE-GAN baseline in this
thesis uses a moments accountant to accumulate the cost of noisy teacher
queries~\cite{pategan}. The private language-model generator uses Opacus
for DP-SGD and privacy accounting~\cite{Yousefpour2021Opacus}. In the recorded run, the privacy
cost is calculated using a PRV accountant, which numerically composes
privacy-loss random variables.

\section{Evaluation Frameworks for Synthetic Data}
\label{sec:bg:eval}

% This thesis adopts a two-pillar evaluation framework: utility and privacy.
% The utility pillar is broad and itself contains two complementary aspects:
% statistical similarity (how well the synthetic distributions resemble the
% real ones) and predictive performance (how well downstream models trained
% on synthetic data behave). Privacy is assessed separately. The literature
% reviewed below populates both pillars.

Beyond individual metrics, several integrated tools package utility and privacy
evaluation for tabular synthetic data. A recent study surveys the 
synthetic-data evaluation ecosystem and
introduces SynthEval. It compares SynthEval with SynthCity, SDMetrics,
TableEvaluator, and DataSynthesizer in terms of configurability,
extensibility, ranking support, and the handling of mixed categorical and
numerical attributes~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}. A common limitation 
of several evaluation tools is that they encode
categorical variables as ordinal integers, which can bias the evaluation
toward numerical data. SynthEval instead evaluates categorical and
numerical variables using type-appropriate distance
functions~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}. These 
frameworks provide general-purpose tabular evaluation and ranking but are not 
tied to any particular generative paradigm or clinical setting. The
unified pipeline assembled in this thesis instead applies a common two-pillar
evaluation across five generators spanning the GAN and LLM paradigms on a single
clinical benchmark (\S\ref{sec:method:evaluation}).

\subsection{Utility - Statistical Similarity to the Real Data}
\label{sec:bg:eval:similarity}

Fidelity metrics can be divided into univariate and multivariate
measures. Univariate measures include the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test,
Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, and moment based comparisons.
Multivariate measures include correlation-matrix similarity, Maximum Mean
Discrepancy, and Wasserstein distance. Together, these metrics assess how
closely synthetic data reproduce the distribution of real
data~\cite{Murtaza2023}. These distributional measures can be complemented by
combining KL divergence with pairwise correlation differences (PCD) to assess
both marginal fidelity and pairwise linear-dependence preservation in synthetic patient
data~\cite{goncalves2020generation}. At the per-dimension level, the Bernoulli
success probability of each dimension can be compared between real and synthetic
data (dimension-wise probability)~\cite{Choi2017MedGAN}. Univariate
fidelity can be summarised through the median Hellinger distance across
variables which is bounded in $[0,1]$~\cite{emam2021optimizing}. A high
univariate score does not by itself guarantee fidelity of the joint structure,
since per-column metrics are computed independently and cannot capture
inter-feature dependencies such as clinical co-occurrence
patterns~\cite{Murtaza2023}. Beyond linear correlation, 
inter-attribute dependence can be quantified through a
mutual-information matrix difference~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}. 
A complementary, classifier-based notion
of resemblance is \emph{distinguishability}. Real and synthetic 
records are combined, and a binary classifier is
trained to distinguish between them. For equally sized datasets, fidelity
is measured by the mean squared deviation of the predicted probability
from $0.5$ where a value close to zero indicates that the classifier cannot
reliably distinguish synthetic records from real
records~\cite{emam2021optimizing}. In the framework adopted here these
metrics are treated as one branch of utility, since a synthetic dataset that
fails to preserve the statistical structure of the real data cannot reasonably
be expected to support downstream tasks.

\subsection{Utility - Predictive Performance of Downstream Models}
\label{sec:bg:eval:predictive}

Surveys of tabular synthetic-data generators identify model-based utility
evaluation as an important approach. Common examples include
Train-on-Synthetic Test-on-Real (TSTR) and the related TRTR, TRTS, and
TSTS regimes, which assess whether synthetic data support realistic
downstream prediction~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic}. Reported metrics typically include
accuracy, F1, and AUC. Such regimes are widely
employed~\cite{wang2019continuous,yang2019grouped,park2018data,beaulieu2019privacy}.
A multivariate alternative, dimension-wise prediction (DWP), predicts each
dimension from the remaining ones and compares the F1 of classifiers trained on
real versus synthetic data~\cite{Choi2017MedGAN}. This is extended to an
``all-models'' multivariate prediction-accuracy
comparison~\cite{emam2021optimizing}. Together with the statistical-similarity
metrics in the previous subsection, these predictive-performance metrics
constitute the second branch of the utility pillar used throughout the thesis.

\subsection{Privacy Metrics: Distance, Inference, and Linkability}
\label{sec:bg:eval:privacy}

% Single combined section pulling together the privacy-metric paragraph from
% proposal §4 (the long paragraph beginning "Across the synthetic data
% literature").
Across the synthetic data literature, different studies employ diverse
privacy metrics to quantify disclosure risks~\cite{Murtaza2023}. One evaluation framework 
measures membership inference and attribute 
inference for each patient record~\cite{syninhospital}. It also measures privacy gain, which 
captures the reduction in re-identification probability when synthetic 
data are disclosed instead of real data. HealthGAN-based work evaluates privacy through
Nearest-Neighbour Adversarial Accuracy (AA) and privacy loss, and also
simulates membership inference attacks~\cite{yale2020synthesizing}.
Additional metrics have been surveyed, including
identity/attribute disclosure, Distance to Closest Record (DCR), membership
attack success rate, max real-to-synthetic similarity, formulation of
differential privacy, privacy loss, and Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic}.
Privacy approaches can be grouped into integrated mechanisms, post-hoc
metrics, and assumed privacy. Integrated mechanisms use either wrapper
methods, such as DP and Rényi DP, or embedded methods, such as
moments-accountant DP, $\varepsilon$-identifiability, $\ell$-diversity, and
probabilistic $k$-anonymity. Post-hoc metrics include nearest-neighbour,
reproduction, outlier, and distribution-based measures, while assumed
privacy treats protection as inherited or built into the generation
method~\cite{Murtaza2023}.
Membership inference has been simulated by matching real and
synthetic records through distance thresholds (e.g., Hamming
distance~\cite{goncalves2020generation}) and evaluate attribute disclosure by
testing whether unknown attributes can be inferred from nearest synthetic
neighbours. Recent evaluation frameworks consolidate these into ready-to-use
privacy metrics. SynthEval includes several privacy measures. These include
nearest-neighbour adversarial accuracy and its privacy-loss estimate,
membership-inference and attribute-disclosure risks, distance to the
closest record, $\varepsilon$-identifiability risk, and hitting rate. The
hitting rate measures the proportion of real records reproduced within a
specified tolerance~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}.

\subsection{Over-reliance on Synthetic Data and the Limits of Similarity-Based Privacy Evaluation}
\label{sec:bg:eval:overreliance}

Although synthetic data is frequently presented as a privacy-preserving
substitute for real records, recent work cautions against treating it as a
``silver bullet''. Synthesis does not automatically
eliminate disclosure risk, as a synthetic dataset can still leak information
about the individuals from which it was derived~\cite{stadler2022synthetic}.
In particular, an adversary with access to the generative procedure can infer
the presence or absence of individual records and reconstruct sensitive
attributes of known individuals~\cite{houssiau2022tapas}. Overfitting can 
facilitate membership leakage. However,
memorisation may expose individual training examples even when overall
overfitting is limited~\cite{Shokri2017MIA,Carlini2021Extracting}.
It is further argued that the prevailing reliance on similarity-based 
metrics for privacy evaluation is inadequate for two reasons. Such metrics 
do not faithfully reflect privacy guarantees, since inference attacks can 
succeed even when the synthetic data is dissimilar from the real data. 
In addition, publishing similarity-based metrics can itself introduce 
new disclosure risks, such as reconstruction attacks~\cite{kaabachi2025scoping}. These
observations motivate the privacy methodology adopted in this thesis
(\S\ref{sec:eval:privacy}), which quantifies disclosure risk through explicit
membership- and attribute-inference simulations and a formal
differential-privacy guarantee rather than through distance to the real data
alone. This caution is reinforced empirically by SynthEval. Its benchmark
finds that a differentially private generator can still fail
membership-inference and attribute-disclosure tests. Thus a reported 
privacy parameter does not remove the need for implementation auditing 
and empirical attack evaluation~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}.

\section{Aggregated Privacy-Utility Views}
\label{sec:bg:aggregate-views}

The metric families reviewed above are not interpreted in
isolation. Synthetic-data evaluation uses aggregate
privacy-utility views to show how several utility and disclosure-risk
measures interact~\cite{thees2025beyond}. SynthEval, for example, reports multiple utility and
privacy metrics and uses internal ranking to support comparison without
hiding the underlying metric values~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}. More 
recent risk-utility visualisation work treats anonymisation and 
synthesis as a multivariate optimisation problem. In this setting, 
heatmaps, dot plots, composite scatterplots, radial profile charts, 
and parallel coordinate plots can make trade-offs between disclosure risk 
and utility visible simultaneously~\cite{thees2025beyond}.

These aggregate views reduce reliance on any single metric. A generator may
perform well on one utility or privacy measure while performing poorly on
another. When presented alongside the raw per-metric values, visual aggregation
can reveal privacy-utility trade-offs and identify dominated operating
points~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval,thees2025beyond}.

At the same time, aggregate views introduce their own risk of
misinterpretation. Composite scores and scaled plots depend on orientation,
normalisation, and metric selection. If these choices are not made
explicit, they can obscure the reason why one generator appears preferable
to another~\cite{thees2025beyond}. For this reason, the present thesis uses aggregate views only
as interpretive summaries. The raw per-metric values remain the primary
basis for the comparison.

\section{Research Gap and Positioning}
\label{sec:bg:gap}

The literature surveyed in this chapter includes two distinct
model-development strands. GAN-based methods for tabular health-data
synthesis include HealthGAN and the differentially private PATE-GAN
construction~\cite{healthgan,pategan}. Language models have subsequently
been adapted to tabular data through row-as-text serialisation and evaluated
for health-data synthesis~\cite{Borisov2023GReaT,Miletic2024}. However, the
studies considered here provide only partial coverage of the comparison
required in this thesis. The HealthGAN study evaluates HealthGAN against five
non-language-model baselines using utility, resemblance, and privacy
measures~\cite{healthgan}. The Pythia benchmark directly compares
Pythia-based generators with CTGAN but focuses on predictive utility and
does not include a privacy assessment~\cite{Miletic2024}. Taken together,
these studies do not compare GAN-based and language-model-based synthesisers
using a common evaluation of both utility and privacy.

A related methodological concern is the selection of suitable evaluation
measures. Existing reviews conclude that no universal method or metric is
sufficient for evaluating synthetic data and that similarity-based measures
alone do not provide an adequate assessment of
privacy~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,kaabachi2025scoping}. The present thesis
responds by applying a unified evaluation pipeline to five selected
generators on the same medical benchmark. The pipeline reports complementary
measures of statistical similarity, predictive utility, and record-level
privacy, including explicit membership-inference and attribute-inference
attacks. This pipeline addresses the metric-selection question in RQ1,
supports the cross-family comparison in RQ2, and provides a common basis for
contrasting differentially private and non-private methods within each
model family in RQ3.
