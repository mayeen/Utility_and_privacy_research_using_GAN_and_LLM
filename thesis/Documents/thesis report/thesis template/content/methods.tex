\subsection{Selection Rationale}
\label{sec:methods:rationale}

The five generators evaluated in this thesis populate a comparative design
space spanned by two axes: the generative paradigm --- contrasting
generative adversarial networks (GANs) with large language models (LLMs)
--- and the privacy regime, which contrasts models trained without a
formal guarantee against models trained under differential privacy (DP).
On the LLM side a third axis, model scale, is introduced via a small
(Pythia-70M) and a large (Pythia-1B) non-DP baseline; the DP variant is
applied at the larger scale, for reasons set out in
\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp:why-1b}. This arrangement permits the effect
of formal privacy to be isolated within each paradigm, the two paradigms
to be compared under a common privacy regime, and the LLM-side scale
sensitivity to be characterised. The rationale for each generator is set
out below.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0.25em
  \item \textbf{HealthGAN (non-private GAN baseline).} HealthGAN couples the
        medGAN design with the Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty (WGAN-GP)
        framework and a dedicated transformation pipeline for the mixed
        continuous and categorical attributes characteristic of electronic
        health records. It further introduces Nearest-Neighbour Adversarial
        Accuracy, a metric suite that jointly quantifies resemblance and
        privacy, and reports competitive utility and resemblance relative to
        established baselines while supporting secure model
        export~\cite{healthgan}. These properties make it a suitable
        representative of the non-private GAN paradigm
        (\S\ref{sec:bg:gan:health}).
  \item \textbf{PATE-GAN (differentially private GAN).} PATE-GAN provides
        formal $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP by adapting the Private Aggregation of
        Teacher Ensembles framework~\cite{Papernot2017PATE} to generative
        modelling: an ensemble of teacher discriminators is trained on disjoint
        data partitions, their predictions are combined through a
        differentially private noisy aggregation mechanism, and a student
        discriminator is trained on the resulting labels, so that the generator
        inherits the privacy guarantee by post-processing~\cite{pategan}. It
        thus supplies a GAN-based generator whose privacy budget is explicitly
        controllable and accountable (\S\ref{sec:bg:gan:pategan}).
  \item \textbf{Pythia-70M (non-private LLM, small-scale baseline).} The
        benchmark study of \textcite{Miletic2024} evaluates the Pythia
        family of decoder-only language models for synthetic tabular health
        data using the Tabula framework, in which each table row is
        serialised as a text sequence, and reports that these models can
        match or surpass a strong tabular GAN on train-on-synthetic,
        test-on-real utility. Pythia-70M is included here as the
        small-scale non-private LLM representative
        (\S\ref{sec:bg:llm:tabula}), and, as discussed in
        \S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp:why-1b}, also frames the model-scale
        precondition that motivated migrating the LLM-side DP variant to
        the 1B model.
  \item \textbf{Pythia-1B (non-private LLM, scale anchor).} A larger
        non-private LLM variant is added so that the LLM-side DP comparison
        (Pythia-1B versus Pythia-1B-DP) isolates the cost of DP-SGD at a
        fixed model size, rather than confounding it with the model-scale
        change to 1B. Pythia-1B uses the same Tabula serialisation and the
        same LoRA fine-tuning protocol as Pythia-70M, differing only in
        the underlying decoder-only backbone.
  \item \textbf{Pythia-1B-DP (differentially private LLM, scope extension).}
        Beyond the generators specified in the original proposal, this
        thesis introduces a differentially private variant of Pythia-1B
        trained with differentially private stochastic gradient descent
        (DP-SGD)~\cite{Abadi2016DPSGD}, as implemented in the Opacus
        library~\cite{Yousefpour2021Opacus}. This addition completes the
        design space and enables a within-paradigm assessment of the cost
        of formal privacy for the LLM, paralleling the
        HealthGAN-to-PATE-GAN comparison on the GAN side.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{HealthGAN: WGAN-GP Baseline}
\label{sec:methods:healthgan}

% Code references:
%   - SDV encoding/decoding: ../../healthgan/generators/sdv_converter.py
%   - WGAN-GP training:      ../../healthgan/generators/wgan_for_mac.py

HealthGAN realises the non-private GAN baseline as a Wasserstein GAN with
gradient penalty (WGAN-GP) trained on a fully numeric encoding of the
diabetes dataset~\cite{healthgan}. The raw mixed-type table is first mapped
to a continuous representation bounded in $[0,1]$; a generator and a critic
are then trained adversarially on that representation; and synthetic records
are recovered by inverting the encoding. The subsubsections below describe,
in turn, the encoding pipeline, the network architecture, the training
objective, the training configuration, and the selection of the synthetic
sample carried forward to the comparative evaluation.

\subsubsection{SDV Encoding and Decoding}
\label{sec:methods:healthgan:sdv}

The networks operate not on the raw table but on an encoded representation in
which every column is mapped to the unit interval $[0,1]$, yielding a purely
continuous and bounded input. The encoder dispatches each column to one of
four transformations according to its data type.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0.25em
  \item \textbf{Categorical columns.} Each category is allocated a
        sub-interval of $[0,1]$ whose width equals the category's relative
        frequency in the column. Every observed value is then replaced by a
        draw from a truncated normal distribution supported on the
        corresponding sub-interval, with mean at the interval midpoint and
        standard deviation equal to one sixth of the interval width. The
        ordered map from cumulative interval boundary to category is retained
        as a decoding table.
  \item \textbf{Ordinal columns.} Non-binary integer columns follow the same
        interval-partition scheme, with two modifications that preserve order.
        Integer levels absent from the observed range are reinserted with
        zero count, and additive (Laplace) smoothing with $\alpha = 1$ is
        applied to the level frequencies before the partition is formed, so
        that an unobserved level still receives a non-degenerate
        sub-interval.
  \item \textbf{Binary columns.} By default these are treated as categorical;
        under an optional configuration flag they are instead mapped through
        truncated Beta distributions parameterised by the observed class
        balance.
  \item \textbf{Numeric columns.} Floating-point columns are min--max
        normalised to $[0,1]$, and the per-column minimum and maximum are
        stored for inversion.
\end{itemize}

A configurable set of columns is forced onto the ordinal path even when
stored as floating point, so that low-cardinality coded variables (such as
the binned age field) are not modelled as continuous quantities. Missing
values are resolved before transformation: categorical columns receive a
dedicated placeholder level, ordinal and binary columns are filled with the
column mode, and numeric columns with the column median.

Decoding inverts each transformation. A column with a stored interval map is
decoded by locating the sub-interval that contains the generated value and
emitting the associated category, whereas a min--max column is rescaled to
its original range. Because the generator can emit values marginally outside
$[0,1]$, decoded inputs are first clipped to that interval. A subsequent
snap-to-nearest pass then reconciles any numeric column with the discrete
value set of the real data: a column with at most fifty distinct observed
values is snapped to its nearest valid value, and a high-cardinality
identifier column is clipped to a non-negative integer. This guarantees that
every value in the synthetic table is admissible under the original schema.

\subsubsection{WGAN-GP Architecture}
\label{sec:methods:healthgan:arch}

Both networks are fully connected feed-forward networks whose layer widths
are defined relative to the encoded feature count $F$ and a base width $B$.
For the encoded diabetes table $F = 45$, and the configuration used in this
thesis sets $B = 64$.

The generator transforms a latent noise vector into a synthetic record. Its
input is a $100$-dimensional vector drawn from a standard multivariate normal
distribution, $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_{100})$, which is
passed through three dense layers:
\begin{equation}
  100 \;\to\; 2F~(\mathrm{ReLU}) \;\to\;
  \mathrm{round}(1.5F)~(\mathrm{ReLU}) \;\to\; F~(\mathrm{sigmoid}),
\end{equation}
that is, hidden widths of $90$ and $68$ units and an output of $45$ units.
The terminal sigmoid constrains every output to $[0,1]$, matching the range
of the encoded representation.

The critic maps a record to an unbounded scalar score through four dense
layers:
\begin{equation}
  F \;\to\; B~(\mathrm{LeakyReLU}) \;\to\; 2B~(\mathrm{LeakyReLU})
  \;\to\; 4B~(\mathrm{LeakyReLU}) \;\to\; 1,
\end{equation}
with hidden widths of $64$, $128$, and $256$ units and a single linear output
unit. Consistent with the Wasserstein formulation, the output is left
unactivated: the critic estimates a real-valued distance rather than a
classification probability.

\subsubsection{Training Loss: Wasserstein with Gradient Penalty}
\label{sec:methods:healthgan:loss}

The networks are trained under the Wasserstein GAN objective with gradient
penalty~\cite{Arjovsky2017WGAN, Gulrajani2017WGANGP}. Let $G$ denote the
generator and $D$ the critic, and let $P_r$ and $P_g$ denote the real and
generated data distributions, respectively. The critic and generator losses
are
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{L}_D &= \mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x}\sim P_g}\!\left[D(\tilde{x})\right]
    - \mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_r}\!\left[D(x)\right]
    + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GP}}, \\
  \mathcal{L}_G &= -\,\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x}\sim P_g}\!\left[D(\tilde{x})\right].
\end{align}
The first two terms of $\mathcal{L}_D$ estimate the Wasserstein-1 distance
between the real and generated distributions. The gradient-penalty term
enforces the $1$-Lipschitz constraint that the Wasserstein formulation
requires of the critic, by penalising departures of the critic's
input-gradient norm from unity at points interpolated between real and
generated samples:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GP}} =
    \mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}\sim P_{\hat{x}}}\!\left[
      \bigl(\lVert \nabla_{\hat{x}} D(\hat{x}) \rVert_2 - 1\bigr)^2
    \right],
  \qquad
  \hat{x} = \epsilon\,x + (1-\epsilon)\,\tilde{x},
  \quad \epsilon \sim U(0,1),
\end{equation}
where each interpolated point $\hat{x}$ is formed with an independent uniform
coefficient $\epsilon$ drawn per sample. The penalty weight is fixed at
$\lambda = 10$, the value recommended by \textcite{Gulrajani2017WGANGP}. This
gradient-penalty mechanism replaces the weight clipping of the original
Wasserstein GAN~\cite{Arjovsky2017WGAN}; its incompatibility with the
per-example gradient clipping required by differentially private training is
the architectural property examined in \S\ref{sec:methods:healthgan:nodp}.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameters and Training Configuration}
\label{sec:methods:healthgan:hp}

The training configuration follows the values fixed in the WGAN-GP
implementation. Each training epoch performs $n_{\mathrm{critic}} = 5$ critic
updates followed by a single generator update, so that the critic --- the
only component that directly processes real records --- is exposed to the
real data five times as often as the generator. Both networks are optimised
with Adam ($\eta = 10^{-4}$, $\beta_1 = 0.5$, $\beta_2 = 0.9$). The mini-batch
size is derived from the training-set size as
$\lfloor N_{\mathrm{train}}/n_{\mathrm{critic}}\rfloor$, rounded down to the
nearest multiple of $100$, which yields $11{,}400$ for the
$N_{\mathrm{train}} = 57{,}214$ encoded training records; the large batch
follows HealthGAN's design intent of reliably capturing rare clinical
values~\cite{healthgan}. Training runs for $100{,}000$ epochs, and the critic
loss on the held-out test partition ($N_{\mathrm{test}} = 14{,}304$ records)
is logged every $1{,}000$ epochs as a convergence diagnostic. On completion,
the trained generator is sampled to produce ten synthetic files, each
containing $14{,}304$ records to match the size of the test partition.

\subsubsection{Selection of the Synthetic Sample}
\label{sec:methods:healthgan:variants}

Sampling the trained generator is stochastic: a single training run yields
three interchangeable synthetic files (indexed $0$, $3$, and $6$) drawn
from the same fitted model. These files are not identical, as each is an
independent draw and therefore exhibits sampling variation in its marginal
and joint statistics. To base the cross-method comparison on a justified
rather than an arbitrary sample, the candidates were evaluated against the
real test partition along two complementary axes: marginal fidelity,
quantified by the mean Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic across columns, and
dependency fidelity, quantified by the mean absolute difference between the
synthetic and real pairwise correlation matrices, with a principal-component
projection used as a visual cross-check of distributional overlap
(Appendix~\ref{app:hg-variants}).

Table~\ref{tab:hg-variants} reports the two fidelity measures for the three
candidates. The spread is negligible: the mean KS statistic ranges over only
$0.0477$--$0.0484$ (a span of $0.0007$) and the mean correlation difference
over $0.0336$--$0.0342$ (a span of $0.0006$). Similarity to the real data is
therefore carried near-identically across the independent draws, and no
candidate is meaningfully better than the others. The combined fidelity rank
places draws $0$ and $3$ jointly first (average rank $1.50$): draw $3$ has
the marginally lower KS statistic, while draw $0$ has the lowest correlation
difference. Given this tie and the negligible overall spread, draw $0$ was
carried forward to the cross-method comparison as a representative sample;
the choice has no material effect on any downstream result.

\subsubsection{Rationale for Excluding a DP-Augmented HealthGAN Variant}
\label{sec:methods:healthgan:nodp}

% Migrated from thesis_conversation.md scope-decision note.
A natural question is whether differential privacy could be applied
directly to the HealthGAN training loop --- for instance by replacing
the Adam optimiser of the critic with a DP-SGD variant
\cite{Abadi2016DPSGD} via \texttt{tensorflow\_privacy}. This thesis does
not pursue that direction; the DP-GAN comparison is instead realised
through the architecturally distinct PATE-GAN model
(\S\ref{sec:methods:pategan}). Four technical considerations motivate
this choice.

First, the gradient-penalty term that distinguishes WGAN-GP from
weight-clipping WGAN \cite{Arjovsky2017WGAN, Gulrajani2017WGANGP} is
itself a function of real data, as it is computed over interpolated
samples drawn between real and generated points. Its gradient cannot be
cleanly clipped on a per-example basis as DP-SGD requires, so
accommodating DP-SGD would necessitate dropping the gradient penalty
and reverting to weight-clipping Wasserstein training, sacrificing the
architectural property that motivated the WGAN-GP variant in the first
place. Second, the training protocol applies five critic updates per
generator update; because the critic is the sole component that
directly processes real records, this $5{:}1$ ratio implies the privacy
budget would be consumed five times faster than in a configuration
performing one private gradient computation per logical batch, as is
the case in the Pythia-DP setting (\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp}).
Third, the injection of Gaussian noise into critic gradients --- the
core operation of DP-SGD --- is well documented to exacerbate the mode
collapse and non-convergence that already characterise GAN training on
tabular health data, with empirical results in the DP-GAN literature
\cite{Xie2018DPGAN, pategan} demonstrating sharp utility cliffs at
budgets of $\varepsilon \leq 10$. Fourth, the HealthGAN implementation
relies on TensorFlow~1 compatibility mode with eager execution
disabled, and the per-example gradient computation required by
\texttt{tensorflow\_privacy} is substantially slower under graph
execution, rendering the $100{,}000$-epoch training schedule
computationally prohibitive within the resource envelope of this
project.

Beyond these technical obstacles, the experimental design already
provides a controlled DP-GAN baseline through PATE-GAN, which attains
formal $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP through a fundamentally different
mechanism --- noisy aggregation of teacher discriminators
\cite{pategan, Papernot2017PATE} --- rather than by injecting noise
into the critic's gradients. The HealthGAN $\to$ PATE-GAN pairing
therefore isolates the cost of formal DP on the GAN side without
confounding it with an unstable adaptation of an architecture that was
not designed for per-example gradient clipping. HealthGAN is retained
as the non-private GAN utility baseline.

\subsection{PATE-GAN: Differentially Private GAN}
\label{sec:methods:pategan}

% Code references:
%   - Pategan/pate_gan.py
%   - Pategan/generate_pategan_synthetic.py

PATE-GAN realises the differentially private GAN baseline by combining a
standard generator with a student discriminator whose only access to the
real training data is mediated by an ensemble of teacher discriminators
trained on disjoint partitions of that data~\cite{pategan,Papernot2017PATE}.
Differential privacy is enforced exclusively at the teacher-to-student
interface through Laplace-noised majority voting, so that all subsequent
updates to the student and the generator are post-processing of a
$(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP signal and therefore inherit the same privacy
guarantee. Unlike the SDV-encoded representation used by HealthGAN
(\S\ref{sec:methods:healthgan:sdv}), PATE-GAN operates directly on the
feature-engineered table: each of the $D = 45$ columns of the preprocessed
training split is min--max scaled to $[0,1]$, the generator is trained on
that representation, and synthetic outputs are inverted by undoing the
min--max scaling, clipping to $[0,1]$, and rounding columns whose observed
range consists of integer endpoints.

\subsubsection{Teacher--Student Framework}
\label{sec:methods:pategan:teacher}

The training data is partitioned uniformly at random into $k = 10$ disjoint
subsets of equal size $\lfloor N_{\mathrm{train}} / k \rfloor = 5{,}721$
records each. At every outer training round the implementation refits, from
scratch, one logistic-regression teacher per partition; that is, the
teachers are linear logistic-regression classifiers rather than neural
networks. Each teacher is trained on a balanced binary
discrimination task in which its assigned real partition supplies the
positive class and an equal-sized batch sampled from the current generator
supplies the negative class. Because each real record is seen by exactly
one teacher, the privacy cost of teacher training is bounded; the student,
which is the only component that subsequently produces gradients for the
generator, never observes the real data directly. The student is a small
feed-forward network (architecture in \S\ref{sec:methods:pategan:arch})
whose labels are obtained from the teacher ensemble through the noisy
aggregation mechanism of \S\ref{sec:methods:pategan:laplace}, and the
generator is updated against the student through a Wasserstein-style
objective with weight clipping~\cite{Arjovsky2017WGAN}.

\subsubsection{PATE Voting with the Laplace Mechanism}
\label{sec:methods:pategan:laplace}

For a generator-produced sample $\tilde{x}$, the implemented aggregation
counts the binary votes of the $k$ teachers,
\begin{equation}
  n_0(\tilde{x}) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{k} \mathbf{1}\bigl[T_t(\tilde{x}) = 0\bigr],
  \qquad
  n_1(\tilde{x}) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{k} \mathbf{1}\bigl[T_t(\tilde{x}) = 1\bigr],
\end{equation}
draws an independent Laplace noise term $\eta \sim \mathrm{Lap}(0, \lambda)$
per query, and assigns the supervised label
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}(\tilde{x}) \;=\;
  \mathbf{1}\!\left[\,
    \frac{n_1(\tilde{x}) + \eta}{\,n_0(\tilde{x}) + n_1(\tilde{x})\,}
    \;>\; \tfrac{1}{2}
  \right].
  \label{eq:pategan-aggregation}
\end{equation}
The Laplace scale $\lambda$ controls the noise injected at each query:
larger $\lambda$ gives stronger per-query privacy but a noisier
supervisory signal for the student. The implementation used in this
thesis sets $\lambda = 1.0$. Equation~(\ref{eq:pategan-aggregation})
differs slightly from the more familiar noisy-argmax formulation of the
PATE framework~\cite{Papernot2017PATE}, in which independent Laplace
noise is added to every class count; the codebase shipped with the
original PATE-GAN study~\cite{pategan} adds noise only to the count of
the positive class and thresholds the resulting noisy ratio, and this
thesis preserves that behaviour without modification so that the privacy
analysis of the original paper applies directly.

\subsubsection{Privacy Accounting via the Moments Accountant}
\label{sec:methods:pategan:accountant}

The privacy cost of each noisy aggregation is tracked through the moments
accountant introduced by~\textcite{Abadi2016DPSGD} and adapted by
PATE-GAN~\cite{pategan}, with $L = 20$ tracked moment orders. Let
$|n_0 - n_1|$ denote the vote-margin of a single query and define
\begin{equation}
  q \;=\;
  \frac{2 + \lambda\,|n_0 - n_1|}
       {4\,\exp\!\bigl(\lambda\,|n_0 - n_1|\bigr)},
\end{equation}
which corresponds to the bound on the per-query Rényi divergence used in
the data-dependent PATE accountant~\cite{pategan,Mironov2017RDP}. For each
moment order $l \in \{0, \dots, L-1\}$ the accumulator $\alpha_l$ is
updated by
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_l \;\leftarrow\; \alpha_l \;+\;
    \min\!\Bigl(\, 2\,\lambda^{2}\,(l+1)(l+2),
    \;\;
    \log\!\bigl[
      (1-q)\bigl(\tfrac{1-q}{1 - q\,\exp(2\lambda)}\bigr)^{\,l+1}
      + q\,\exp\!\bigl(2\lambda(l+1)\bigr)
    \bigr]
    \Bigr),
\end{equation}
which selects, per order, the tighter of the data-independent and the
data-dependent bound. After every outer round (one teacher refit, $n_s$
student updates, and one generator update) the implementation converts the
accumulated moments back into an $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ budget via
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\varepsilon} \;=\;
  \min_{l \in \{0, \dots, L-1\}}
    \frac{\alpha_l + \log\!\bigl(1/\delta\bigr)}{\,l+1\,}.
  \label{eq:pategan-epshat}
\end{equation}
Training proceeds while $\hat{\varepsilon}$ remains below the target
$\varepsilon$, and terminates as soon as the inequality is violated; the
final synthetic dataset is drawn from the generator state at termination.
The accountant therefore controls the number of training rounds adaptively
rather than through a fixed epoch budget.

\subsubsection{Architecture}
\label{sec:methods:pategan:arch}

Both the generator and the student are fully connected feed-forward
networks whose widths scale with the feature dimension $D = 45$ of the
preprocessed table. The latent input is drawn from the uniform
distribution, $z \sim \mathcal{U}([-1, 1]^{D})$, and is passed through
three dense layers with hidden width $4D = 180$:
\begin{equation}
  D \;\to\; 4D~(\mathrm{tanh}) \;\to\; 4D~(\mathrm{tanh})
  \;\to\; D~(\mathrm{sigmoid}),
\end{equation}
so that the generator output is constrained to $[0,1]^{D}$ in line with
the min--max scaled training representation. The student maps a record
to an unbounded scalar score through two dense layers,
\begin{equation}
  D \;\to\; D~(\mathrm{ReLU}) \;\to\; 1,
\end{equation}
with a single linear output unit and no terminal activation, consistent
with the Wasserstein critic formulation~\cite{Arjovsky2017WGAN}. All
weights are initialised with Xavier scaling, and the teachers are
linear logistic-regression models with default regularisation as
described in \S\ref{sec:methods:pategan:teacher}.

\subsubsection{Privacy Budget and Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:methods:pategan:hp}

The student and generator are both optimised with RMSProp at learning
rate $\eta = 10^{-4}$. The student is trained under the Wasserstein
formulation with hard weight clipping: after every gradient step its
parameters are projected back into the box $[-0.01, 0.01]$, which
enforces the $1$-Lipschitz constraint required of the
critic~\cite{Arjovsky2017WGAN}. The target privacy budget is
$(\varepsilon, \delta) = (5.0,\,10^{-5})$, matched to the budget used
for the Pythia-1B-DP run (\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp:epsilon}) so that
the GAN-side and LLM-side DP comparisons in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:gan-toggle} and \S\ref{sec:results:rq3:llm-toggle}
are reported at a common $\varepsilon$. Training runs until the
moments-accountant estimate $\hat{\varepsilon}$ from
Equation~(\ref{eq:pategan-epshat}) reaches this target. After
termination, one synthetic batch of size $N_{\mathrm{train}} = 57{,}214$
is drawn from the generator and inverted from the min--max
representation to the original feature space. Table~\ref{tab:pategan-hp}
summarises the configuration.

\subsection{Pythia-70M: Non-DP LLM (Small-Scale Baseline)}
\label{sec:methods:pythia}

% Code references:
%   - pythia/pythia_tabular.py
%   - pythia/generate_pythia_synthetic.py

Pythia-70M realises the non-private LLM baseline at the small end of the
scale axis. The pipeline serialises each tabular row as a single text
sequence with a class-label prefix, fine-tunes the base model on those
sequences using low-rank adaptation, and recovers a synthetic table by
prompting the trained model with the same class prefix and parsing the
generated text back into typed values under the source schema. The
subsubsections below describe the base model, the row-to-text
serialisation, the column-level schema captured for postprocessing,
the LoRA fine-tuning configuration, the class-conditioned generation
procedure, and the schema-aware parsing and retry logic that converts
free-form model output into a validated synthetic dataframe.

\subsubsection{Base Model}
\label{sec:methods:pythia:base}

The base model is Pythia-70M, a decoder-only transformer with $70$M
parameters drawn from the open-weights Pythia
suite~\cite{Biderman2023Pythia}. The choice of the smallest Pythia
variant is dictated by training-cost considerations: a $70$M parameter
model fine-tunes on commodity GPU hardware within minutes per epoch,
making it a practical entry point for the LLM family.
\textcite{Miletic2024} report that Pythia models can match or surpass
strong tabular GAN baselines on train-on-synthetic, test-on-real utility
when applied to electronic-health-record tables using row-as-text
serialisation, which motivates including the smallest variant as a
non-private baseline rather than excluding it on capacity grounds.
Tokenisation uses the model's native byte-pair-encoded vocabulary; the
end-of-sequence token is reused as the padding token when the
pretrained tokeniser lacks an explicit one, and the padding side is set
to the left to preserve the structure of generation prompts.

\subsubsection{Tabular-to-Text Serialisation}
\label{sec:methods:pythia:serialisation}

Each row of the preprocessed training table is serialised into a single
text sequence of the form
\begin{equation*}
  \mathrm{Class}\_\langle L\rangle ~|~ c_1 = v_1 ~|~ c_2 = v_2 ~|~ \dots ~|~ c_K = v_K,
\end{equation*}
where $\langle L \rangle$ is the integer class label drawn from the
binary readmission target column, $c_i$ is the $i$-th column name in
the original dataframe order, and $v_i$ is the textual representation
of that column's value in the row. Scalar values are formatted
deterministically: integers retain their textual decimal form,
floating-point values are formatted with $15$ significant digits,
booleans are mapped to $\{0, 1\}$, and missing values are emitted as
the literal string ``NA''. The class prefix is fixed at the head of
the sequence so that conditional generation can later be triggered by
pinning that prefix to the desired label
(\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia:generation}).

\subsubsection{Schema Derivation}
\label{sec:methods:pythia:schema}

Before training, a per-column schema is derived from the source
dataframe and retained for use during parsing, coercion, validation, and
postprocessing of generated outputs. For every column the schema
records: the original pandas dtype; whether the column is numeric;
the observed minimum and maximum (numeric columns only); whether all
observed values are integer-valued (the \emph{integer-coded} flag);
the full sorted list of distinct observed values when the column's
cardinality is at most $25$ (the \emph{discrete numeric support},
applied to ordinal-coded columns such as age decile, diagnosis
groupings, and binary medication flags); the full list of distinct
string categories for non-numeric columns; and the column mode used as
a schema-safe fallback when a generated field cannot be parsed.

\subsubsection{LoRA Fine-tuning}
\label{sec:methods:pythia:lora}

The base Pythia weights are kept frozen and adapted through low-rank
adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{Hu2022LoRA}. The LoRA configuration uses rank
$r = 8$, scaling factor $\alpha = 16$, dropout $0.05$, no bias
adaptation, and the causal-language-modelling task type, so that only
the injected low-rank matrices receive gradient updates. The corpus is
tokenised without padding and dynamically padded per mini-batch to a
multiple of eight by a standard causal-LM data collator (no masked-LM
objective). Training runs for $10$ epochs at per-device batch size $8$,
learning rate $\eta = 2 \times 10^{-5}$, and maximum sequence length
$512$, with half-precision arithmetic on CUDA-capable devices. The
mean training loss per epoch is recorded, the trainable parameters are
snapshotted whenever an epoch achieves a new minimum loss, and the
lowest-loss snapshot is restored at the end of training; sampling for
generation is performed from this best-epoch state, so that transient
over-fitting in the final epochs does not degrade synthetic output. The
fine-tuned model is moved into evaluation mode before generation.

\subsubsection{Class-Conditioned Generation}
\label{sec:methods:pythia:generation}

Generation is performed class-by-class so that the synthetic table
preserves the per-class row counts of the source split. For each
target class $\ell \in \{0, 1\}$, the model is prompted with a class
prefix of the form $\mathrm{Class}\_\ell ~|~ c_1 =$, where $c_1$ is
the name of the first non-target column under the dataframe's column
order, and is left to autoregressively complete the remainder of the
row. Sampling uses nucleus sampling with temperature $0.8$ and
top-$p = 0.95$, with $512$ maximum new tokens, a minimum-new-tokens
floor of $64$ to discourage truncated rows, and the model's key--value
cache enabled. The required class label is enforced after parsing
rather than inferred from the generation, removing any dependence of
the assigned label on the model's own output
(\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia:parsing}).

\subsubsection{Schema-Aware Parsing, Coercion, and Retry}
\label{sec:methods:pythia:parsing}

Each decoded text is normalised by stripping content preceding the
class prefix and extracting key--value pairs with a regular expression
that captures alphanumeric (and underscore- or hyphen-bearing) keys to
the left of an ``='' sign and the corresponding free-form values to
the right, terminated by the pipe separator or by a line break.
Duplicate keys are resolved by retaining the first occurrence. A
generated row is admitted to the coercion stage only if it supplies at
least $\max\bigl(3,\,\lceil 0.1 \cdot K_{\mathrm{nt}} \rceil\bigr)$
distinct non-target keys, where $K_{\mathrm{nt}}$ is the number of
non-target columns in the schema. For the $44$ non-target columns of
the preprocessed diabetes table this lower bound evaluates to four
keys; rows that fall short are rejected as parse failures and the
attempt is retried.

Coercion enforces schema compliance on a per-column basis. Numeric
fields are cast to floating point, clipped to the column's
$[\min, \max]$ envelope, snapped to the nearest value of the
discrete support (when the column is low-cardinality), and rounded to
the nearest integer within bounds (when the column is integer-coded).
String fields are checked against the recorded category set; an
unrecognised category is replaced with the column mode. Any column
missing from the parsed output is filled with a schema-safe default:
the column mode where defined, otherwise the first value of the
discrete support, otherwise the midpoint of the numeric range. The
target column is always overwritten with the externally enforced
class label rather than the model's emitted value, guaranteeing a
one-to-one match between prompt class and recorded class.

Generation for one class proceeds in mini-batches of size equal to the
training batch size until the required row count is reached or an
attempt budget of $8 \cdot n_{\mathrm{rows}}$ generations is exhausted
(an effective per-row retry budget of eight). If the budget is
exhausted with the row count short of target, the shortfall is first
filled by resampling, with replacement, from the rows already accepted
for that class; only if no row at all was accepted is the synthetic
table topped up with rows drawn uniformly from the schema-recorded
support of each column (a last-resort path that was never reached on
the diabetes runs reported here). The generated rows for both classes
are concatenated, shuffled with a fixed seed, and pushed through a
final dataframe-level cast: every column is re-clipped to its recorded
range, snapped to its discrete support, rounded if integer-coded, and
re-cast to its original type. A structural validator then rejects any
output that does not have the correct column order, the correct row
count, no missing values, a binary target column, and within-range
values for all numeric columns before the synthetic table is written
to disk together with a metadata sidecar covering parsing diagnostics
and the per-class acceptance statistics.

\subsection{Pythia-1B: Non-DP LLM (Scale Anchor)}
\label{sec:methods:pythia-1b}

% Code references:
%   - pythia/pythia_tabular.py (run at --model_name EleutherAI/pythia-1b)
%   - pythia/generate_pythia_synthetic.py

A second non-private LLM variant is trained at a larger scale to serve as
the non-DP anchor of the LLM-side DP toggle
(\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp}). Pythia-1B uses the identical Tabula
serialisation (\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia:serialisation}), schema-derivation
pipeline (\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia:schema}), LoRA fine-tuning configuration
(\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia:lora}), class-conditioned generation procedure
(\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia:generation}), and schema-aware parsing logic
(\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia:parsing}) as the 70M variant; the only material
difference is the underlying decoder-only backbone, scaled from the
$70$M-parameter Pythia variant to the $1$B-parameter Pythia
variant~\cite{Biderman2023Pythia}. Including Pythia-1B alongside the smaller 70M
baseline serves two purposes. First, it isolates the cost of DP-SGD in the
RQ3 within-family comparison (Pythia-1B versus Pythia-1B-DP) from the
confounding effect of a simultaneous model-scale change. Second, it
positions an LLM-side scale axis in the RQ2 landscape view, enabling a
qualitative read of how the LLM family's privacy--utility profile shifts
with model size at a matched preprocessing and fine-tuning configuration.

\subsection{Pythia-1B-DP: Differentially Private LLM}
\label{sec:methods:pythia-dp}

% Code references:
%   - pythia/pythia_tabular_dp.py
%   - pythia/generate_pythia_synthetic_dp.py

\subsubsection{Rationale for Operationalising DP-SGD at the 1B Scale}
\label{sec:methods:pythia-dp:why-1b}

An initial attempt to apply DP-SGD to Pythia-70M at $\varepsilon = 5.0$
failed to train usefully: the per-example Gaussian noise injected by
Opacus dominated the gradient signal, training loss did not descend below
the random-token baseline, and the resulting samples failed schema-aware
parsing at a rate that prevented an evaluable synthetic table from being
assembled. Scaling the LLM-side DP variant to Pythia-1B, with all other
hyperparameters held fixed, restored stable convergence under the same
$(\varepsilon = 5.0, \delta = 10^{-5})$ budget. This outcome is consistent
with the observation of \textcite{Li2022LargeDP}, who report that larger
LLMs tolerate DP noise more gracefully, and motivates the asymmetric LLM
lineup: the LLM-side DP toggle is operationalised at the 1B scale, while
the 70M baseline is retained only as a non-DP small-scale reference in
the RQ2 landscape view. The empirical failure itself is reported as a
finding in \S\ref{sec:results:rq3:pythia-70m-failure}.

\subsubsection{DP-SGD via Opacus}
\label{sec:methods:pythia-dp:opacus}

Differential privacy is enforced through differentially private
stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD)~\cite{Abadi2016DPSGD}, with the
Opacus library~\cite{Yousefpour2021Opacus} supplying the per-sample
gradient machinery, the noise calibration, and the privacy
accountant. The pipeline follows the parameter-efficient framework of
\textcite{Yu2022DPFT}: the base Pythia weights are frozen and
differential privacy is applied exclusively to the LoRA adapter
parameters, so that by the post-processing property of differential
privacy the resulting generator is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP with
respect to the private training corpus. After the LoRA adapter is
attached, the model is passed through a module-validation step that
substitutes any sub-module incompatible with per-sample gradient
computation (for example, certain normalisation layers) with an
equivalent Opacus-supported layer. The base optimiser is the Adam-W
algorithm restricted to the trainable adapter parameters; the
optimiser, the model, and the data loader are then handed to
Opacus's privacy engine, which (i) replaces the optimiser with a
DP-SGD variant that injects Gaussian noise into clipped per-sample
gradients, (ii) wraps the model so that per-sample gradients are
materialised during the backward pass, and (iii) calibrates the
Gaussian noise multiplier from the user-supplied
$(\varepsilon, \delta, \text{epochs})$ triplet using its built-in
accountant.

\subsubsection{Per-Example Gradient Clipping}
\label{sec:methods:pythia-dp:clipping}

The DP-SGD optimiser computes a per-sample gradient $g_i$ for every
example $i$ in the logical batch and clips it to a fixed $\ell_2$ norm
$C$, where the clipping bound is set to $C = 1.0$ in this thesis:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{g}_i \;=\; g_i \,\Big/\, \max\!\bigl(1, \lVert g_i \rVert_{2} / C\bigr).
\end{equation}
The clipped gradients are summed across the logical batch, a single
Gaussian noise draw of standard deviation $\sigma\,C$ is added, and the
result is divided by the logical batch size to form the noisy gradient
estimate used by the AdamW update. The Gaussian noise multiplier
$\sigma$ is selected automatically by Opacus from the target
$(\varepsilon, \delta)$ budget and the planned number of epochs. To
keep per-sample gradients well defined under Opacus, the LoRA dropout
parameter is set to zero in the DP path (versus $0.05$ in the non-DP
path of \S\ref{sec:methods:pythia:lora}); LoRA rank, scaling factor,
target task type, and bias configuration are otherwise unchanged.

\subsubsection{Effective Batch Size}
\label{sec:methods:pythia-dp:batch}

The privacy guarantee depends on the \emph{logical} batch size that
the accountant sees, which is taken to be the effective batch size
$B_{\mathrm{eff}} = B_{\mathrm{phys}} \times G$, where
$B_{\mathrm{phys}}$ is the per-device physical batch size and $G$ is
the gradient-accumulation factor. The values used in this thesis are
$B_{\mathrm{phys}} = 32$ and $G = 16$, giving a logical batch size of
$B_{\mathrm{eff}} = 512$. Logical batches are streamed through
Opacus's memory-managed loader, which transparently splits each
logical batch into physical micro-batches of size $B_{\mathrm{phys}}$
so that per-sample gradient memory remains bounded; the privacy
accountant is exposed only to the logical-batch sampling rate
$q = B_{\mathrm{eff}} / N_{\mathrm{train}}$ used in its calibration of
the noise multiplier.

\subsubsection{Privacy Budget and Achieved Epsilon}
\label{sec:methods:pythia-dp:epsilon}

The target privacy budget is $(\varepsilon, \delta) = (5.0,\,10^{-5})$
across all DP-enabled Pythia runs, with $\delta$ chosen well below
$1 / N_{\mathrm{train}}$ as standard practice~\cite{Abadi2016DPSGD}.
Opacus calibrates the Gaussian noise multiplier $\sigma$ from this
budget together with the number of training epochs and the sampling
rate $q$ defined in \S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp:batch}. After
training, the accountant is queried for the realised privacy cost at
the chosen $\delta$ using Opacus's privacy-random-variable accountant;
the realised $\varepsilon$, the noise multiplier $\sigma$, the
per-epoch loss trajectory, the best-epoch index, and the recorded
sampling rate are serialised into a metadata sidecar alongside the
synthetic table and are reported verbatim in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. The same matched budget anchors the GAN-side
DP comparison through PATE-GAN (\S\ref{sec:methods:pategan:hp}), so
that the GAN-family and LLM-family within-family DP toggles in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:gan-toggle} and
\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:llm-toggle} are read against a common
$\varepsilon$.

\subsubsection{Differences from the Non-DP Pipeline}
\label{sec:methods:pythia-dp:diff}

The DP variant deliberately deviates from the non-DP pipeline of
\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia} only where required by the DP-SGD
machinery; all serialisation, schema-derivation, sampling, parsing,
and validation logic is shared. The differences are:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0.25em
  \item \textbf{Training loop.} The high-level training driver is
        replaced by a manual epoch loop over an Opacus-wrapped data
        loader, so that per-sample gradient materialisation, gradient
        clipping, Gaussian noise injection, and privacy accounting
        can be inserted at the right point in the update sequence.
        Mean-loss aggregation and best-epoch snapshotting are
        reimplemented to match the behaviour of the non-DP callback.
  \item \textbf{LoRA dropout.} Set to $0.0$ in the DP path versus
        $0.05$ in the non-DP path, because per-sample gradient
        tracking under Opacus requires deterministic forward passes
        through stochastic regularisers
        (\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp:clipping}).
  \item \textbf{Batch sizing.} The non-DP path trains at per-device
        batch size $8$ with no accumulation
        (\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia:lora}); the DP path uses a
        physical batch size of $32$ with gradient accumulation factor
        $16$, yielding the logical batch size $512$ that the privacy
        accountant operates on. The increased logical batch size is
        load-bearing: smaller sampling rates would either require a
        larger noise multiplier or fail to reach the target
        $\varepsilon$ within the same number of epochs.
  \item \textbf{Memory footprint.} Per-sample gradient tracking
        multiplies the gradient-memory cost by the physical batch
        size, which is the dominant runtime constraint on the DP
        path; Opacus's memory-managed loader mitigates this by
        running the physical micro-batches sequentially within each
        logical batch.
  \item \textbf{Inference unwrap.} Opacus wraps the model in a
        per-sample-gradient module that intercepts the backward pass;
        the underlying transformer is extracted from this wrapper
        before sampling so that the standard generation routine
        dispatches to the inference path rather than the per-sample
        gradient path.
\end{itemize}

Several cross-cutting properties of the design space are worth
re-stating explicitly. First, the privacy mechanism varies not only in
its presence or absence but also in its locus: PATE-GAN injects
calibrated Laplace noise at the teacher-to-student labelling
interface~\cite{pategan,Papernot2017PATE}, whereas Pythia-1B-DP injects
Gaussian noise into clipped per-sample gradients of the LoRA adapter
under the DP-SGD framework~\cite{Abadi2016DPSGD} as specialised to
parameter-efficient fine-tuning by~\textcite{Yu2022DPFT}. Both
mechanisms are calibrated to a matched target privacy budget of
$(\varepsilon, \delta) = (5.0,\,10^{-5})$, which is what makes the
within-family DP toggles in \S\ref{sec:results:rq3:gan-toggle} and
\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:llm-toggle} comparable across families. Second,
the LLM-side generators share an identical row-to-text serialisation,
schema-aware parsing pipeline, and LoRA configuration; the only
material differences across the three Pythia variants are the
underlying decoder-only backbone (70M vs.\ 1B parameters) and, for
the DP variant, the gradient-clipping, noise-injection, and
adapter-dropout adjustments demanded by the per-sample-gradient
machinery (\S\ref{sec:methods:pythia-dp:diff}). Third, the GAN-side
generators are realised on a TensorFlow~1.x compatibility stack
inherited from the original
HealthGAN~\cite{healthgan} and PATE-GAN~\cite{pategan} codebases,
whereas the LLM-side generators run on a PyTorch + Hugging Face
Transformers stack with LoRA~\cite{Hu2022LoRA} and, in the DP path,
Opacus~\cite{Yousefpour2021Opacus}. This software-stack asymmetry has
no bearing on the comparative privacy--utility analysis in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, but it does inform the discussion of
practical deployment cost in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.
