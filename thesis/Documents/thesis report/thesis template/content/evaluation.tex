\section{Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:method:evaluation}

This section operationalises the utility and privacy concepts introduced
in Chapter~\ref{ch:background} into the concrete metrics used to compare
the five synthetic-data generators. The aim is not to define new
evaluation theory, but to fix a reproducible protocol: every generator is
judged against the same real train/test partitions, the same downstream
models, and the same privacy-audit procedures. The numerical results and
the question of which metrics most clearly separate the generators are
reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\subsection{Two Pillars: Utility and Privacy}
\label{sec:eval:pillars}

Evaluation taxonomies differ in how they position resemblance. Some
medical synthetic-data frameworks treat resemblance, utility, and
privacy as three separate dimensions, whereas recent surveys group
distributional fidelity and downstream usefulness under a broader
utility or data-availability heading while retaining privacy as a
separate concern~\cite{kaabachi2025scoping,shi2025comprehensive}. This
thesis adopts the latter approach at the top level: every generator
is assessed along a \emph{utility} pillar and a \emph{privacy} pillar,
with the two reported separately so that trade-offs between them can be
made explicit. To avoid hiding the distinction between resemblance and
task usefulness, the utility pillar is then decomposed into two
complementary branches. The first, \emph{statistical similarity}
(\S\ref{sec:eval:utility:similarity}), asks whether the synthetic table
reproduces the distributional shape of the real data, both the
per-column marginals and the joint structure across columns. The
second, \emph{predictive performance}
(\S\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive}), asks whether the synthetic table
behaves like the real one when used to train downstream predictive and
survival models. The privacy pillar (\S\ref{sec:eval:privacy}) is
operationalised through instance-level distance metrics and explicit
inference-attack simulations. Section~\ref{sec:eval:aggregate}
introduces the aggregation layer through which the per-metric values
are visualised as a single comparative picture for RQ2 and RQ3.

Two design principles govern the choice of metrics throughout this
chapter. First, the metrics within each branch are deliberately
chosen to be \emph{complementary} in what they measure: a marginal
metric, a joint-structure metric, and a manifold-geometry metric are
all reported in the similarity branch. For instance, because a
generator that scores well on one need not score well on another and
the empirical answer to RQ1 depends on this complementarity. Second,
every metric is reported using the same numerical conventions across
generators: the same train/test split, the same preprocessing and
standardisation choices where applicable, the same downstream
classifiers, and fixed random seeds for stochastic evaluation
components. For the differentially private generators, the target
privacy budget is matched at $(\varepsilon,\delta)=(5.0,10^{-5})$.
This uniformity is what makes the cross-generator comparisons in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} sound. The
empirical question of which metrics actually \emph{discriminate}
between the five generators on the Diabetes 130-US Hospitals benchmark,
and which collapse to a single value, is addressed in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq1:diagnostic}. This per-metric, non-composite
reporting aligns with established frameworks such as
SynthEval~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}.

\subsection{Utility Metrics}
\label{sec:eval:utility}

The same synthetic table is evaluated under both statistical-similarity 
and predictive views. This allows a generator with close marginal 
fits but poor predictive performance to be diagnosed clearly. 
Such a generator preserves univariate structure but not conditional structure.

\subsubsection{Statistical Similarity to the Real Data}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity}

\paragraph{Per-Column Distributional Match:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:dist}
The first diagnostic is purely visual. For each column, the empirical 
density of the real and synthetic values is overlaid on the same axis. 
Kernel-density estimates are used for continuous variables. Side-by-side 
bar charts are used for categorical and binary variables. The
purpose of this diagnostic is to expose obvious failure modes that
single-number summaries hide, such as mode collapse on a dominant
category, distributional bimodality flattened into unimodality, or a
synthetic distribution placed on a range disjoint from the real
support. Following the practice surveyed by
\textcite{hernandez2022synthetic} and \textcite{Murtaza2023}, no
single-number summary is attached to these plots. They are reported in
the results chapter (\S\ref{sec:results:rq2:utility:similarity}) and
in an appendix gallery so that any anomaly visible in the
single-number metrics that follow can be cross-checked at the
column level.

\paragraph{Summary Statistics (Mean, Standard Deviation):}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:stats}
The simplest single-number per-column summaries are the sample mean and 
sample standard deviation of each retained column. These summaries are 
computed separately on the real table and on each synthetic table. Moment-based and
dimension-wise summary comparisons are common in synthetic health and
tabular-data evaluation~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023,lautrup2025syntheval}.
The pair
$(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{real}}, \hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{real}})$ versus
$(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{synth}}, \hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{synth}})$ is
informative even though it ignores higher moments and the entire
joint distribution. Large discrepancies in either statistic indicate
that the generator has placed mass on values that do not match the
column's central tendency or spread, which is a much stronger failure
than a per-shape mismatch that preserves the first two moments. Together
these two statistics summarise \emph{low-order marginal moment fidelity}.
They remain blind to all dependencies between columns and to any shape
information beyond the first two moments. In the implementation, the
medication-flag columns and \emph{num\_procedures} are excluded before
this diagnostic is computed. Any remaining column whose real-data mean
and standard deviation are both zero is then removed as degenerate.

\paragraph{Pairwise Correlation Difference (PCD):}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:pcd}
A single-number summary of the joint linear structure across columns
is based on the Pairwise Correlation Difference proposed by
\textcite{goncalves2020generation} for synthetic patient data. In that
paper, PCD is defined as the Frobenius norm of the difference between
the real and synthetic Pearson correlation matrices. The implementation
used in this thesis reports a normalised variant: the mean absolute
difference over unique off-diagonal column pairs. Let $C^{\mathrm{real}}$ and
$C^{\mathrm{synth}}$ denote the Pearson correlation matrices of the real
and synthetic tables computed over the same column set. The reported
PCD is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PCD}(C^{\mathrm{real}}, C^{\mathrm{synth}})
  \;=\;
  \frac{1}{|L|}
  \sum_{(i,j)\in L} \bigl| C^{\mathrm{real}}_{ij} - C^{\mathrm{synth}}_{ij} \bigr|,
  \qquad
  L = \{(i,j) : i > j\}.
  \label{eq:pcd}
\end{equation}
Here, $L$ contains each unordered column pair exactly once. Smaller
values indicate closer agreement of the joint linear structure. 
The metric represents \emph{joint linear dependency fidelity}. 
It tests whether the synthetic table preserves the pairwise correlation 
pattern among columns. However, it remains blind to non-linear dependency, 
higher-order multivariate structure, and univariate shape distortions that 
leave the linear-dependence pattern intact.
The signed per-pair entries of
$C^{\mathrm{real}} - C^{\mathrm{synth}}$ are also reported as
correlation-difference heatmaps. These heatmaps identifies which
column pairs the generator failed to model and show whether each
correlation is overestimated, underestimated, or sign-flipped.

\paragraph{Kolmogorov-Smirnov Two-Sample Test:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:ks}
The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) two-sample test is a non-parametric
distance between the empirical cumulative distribution functions of
two samples. For a single column with real and synthetic empirical
CDFs $F^{\mathrm{real}}$ and $F^{\mathrm{synth}}$, the KS statistic is
\begin{equation}
  D_{KS}
  \;=\;
  \sup_{x} \bigl| F^{\mathrm{real}}(x) - F^{\mathrm{synth}}(x) \bigr|,
  \label{eq:ks}
\end{equation}
which takes values in $[0, 1]$ and equals zero when the two empirical
distributions coincide. The two-sample KS statistic is reported
per column and averaged across columns to summarise marginal fidelity
in a single number. Averaged across columns, the mean KS reflects 
\emph{worst-case marginal discrepancy}. It is more sensitive to 
distribution-shape errors than the moment-based summaries of the 
previous paragraph. However, because it is computed independently 
per column, it conveys no information about joint structure. The
medication-flag columns are excluded from the aggregation because
their values are concentrated on a single category, making the KS
statistic numerically unstable. KS is the canonical univariate
distance reported in the synthetic-medical-data survey of
\textcite{Murtaza2023}.

\paragraph{PCA-Based Manifold Overlap:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:pca}
A two-dimensional principal-component projection of the real and 
synthetic tables is used as a visual diagnostic of overall manifold 
geometry. Both tables are projected onto a shared coordinate system. 
This follows the use of PCA-based visual plausibility checks in synthetic 
health and tabular-data evaluation~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023,lautrup2025syntheval}.
All columns are first
$z$-score standardised on the combined real-plus-synthetic table so
that no single column dominates the variance decomposition. Principal
components are then estimated on the combined table, and every
record, whether real or synthetic, is projected onto the first two
components and plotted with a generator-specific colour. To keep the
scatter readable on a single page, both the real sample and each
synthetic sample are subsampled to at most $5{,}000$ records before
projection. This diagnostic exposes \emph{whole-distribution geometric overlap},
a dimension that the per-column and pairwise metrics do not address. A
 generator can score reasonably on KS and PCD while
nevertheless concentrating its mass in a region of the principal-axes
plane that the real data does not occupy. Conversely, a wide overlap
on the PCA plot is necessary but not sufficient for distributional
fidelity, so this metric is treated as a visual cross-check rather
than as a primary summary statistic.

\subsubsection{Predictive Performance of Downstream Models}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive}

Predictive utility is evaluated through three complementary diagnostics:
classifier-based downstream performance, feature-importance preservation,
and Cox proportional-hazards agreement. The first diagnostic contains the
train-test regimes, classifier suite, scalar metrics, and ROC analysis.
The latter two check whether the learned signal and clinically
interpretable associations are preserved.

\paragraph{Classifier-Based Downstream Performance:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:tstr}
Predictive utility is evaluated primarily with Train-on-Synthetic,
Test-on-Real (TSTR), a model-based utility protocol introduced
by~\textcite{Esteban2017TSTR} and catalogued in synthetic-data
reviews and evaluation frameworks~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023,lautrup2025syntheval}.
In TSTR, a classifier is trained on the synthetic table and evaluated
on the held-out real test split. Its performance is compared with the
Train-on-Real, Test-on-Real (TRTR) baseline, where the same classifier
is trained on the real training split and evaluated on the same real
test split. The gap between TSTR and TRTR therefore measures how much
downstream predictive utility is lost through synthesis.

TRTS (Train on Real, Test on Synthetic) and TSTS (Train on Synthetic,
Test on Synthetic) are reported as complementary checks. TRTS asks
whether synthetic test records are realistic enough for a real-trained
classifier, while TSTS checks whether the synthetic train and synthetic
test splits are internally consistent. In this thesis, all five active
generators provide synthetic test splits, so TRTS and TSTS are computed
alongside TSTR for each generator.

\paragraph{Classifier Suite:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:classifiers}
Three downstream classifiers are evaluated in parallel, chosen to
span linear, ensemble-tree, and gradient-boosted regimes.
Logistic regression with default $\ell_2$ regularisation and up to
$1{,}000$ optimisation iterations, a random forest with $100$ trees
under default split settings, and gradient boosting with $100$ boosting
rounds. Reporting all three rather than a single ``best'' classifier
limits the risk that a generator's utility ranking is an artefact of
the particular inductive bias of one classifier, a concern raised
in the synthetic-data survey of \textcite{Murtaza2023}. Before
classifier fitting, missing values in any training source are imputed
by per-column median imputation fit on the training partition and
applied to the test partition. This guards against information leakage
from test statistics into the imputation. All classifiers are seeded
with a fixed random state so that the cross-generator comparison is
not perturbed by run-to-run variance within a classifier.

\paragraph{Performance Metrics:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:metrics}
For each (Train Source, Classifier) cell five metrics are reported:
classification accuracy, the binary F1 score of the positive
(readmission) class, the area under the
receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), precision, and
recall, with the positive class defined as the readmission indicator.
The full five-metric set is retained because accuracy alone is
misleading on the moderately imbalanced readmission target. F1 and
the precision/recall pair expose threshold-dependent failure modes
that AUC-ROC averages out, and AUC-ROC in turn reflects
threshold-independent ranking quality that F1 ignores. In short,
accuracy summarises \emph{aggregate correctness of the thresholded
decision rule}, F1 the \emph{balance between false positives and false negatives at
the chosen threshold}, and AUC-ROC the \emph{threshold-independent
ranking quality}. A generator can score well on one of these and
poorly on the others, so they are treated as complementary rather
than as alternative scalarisations of the same underlying quantity.

\paragraph{ROC Curve Analysis:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:roc}
The gradient-boosting classifier is additionally used to generate
ROC-curve overlays for the TRTR baseline and each TSTR generator.
The overlay diagnoses whether a TSTR generator's gap to the baseline 
is concentrated at a particular operating point along the curve. 
It also shows whether the gap is spread uniformly across thresholds. 
This information is collapsed away by the scalar AUC-ROC reported in 
the previous paragraph.

\paragraph{Feature-Importance Preservation:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:fi}
A complementary check on conditional structure is whether the
random-forest variable-importance profile obtained from a synthetic-trained
classifier allocates predictive importance across the engineered features
in the same way as the real-trained reference. Random forests are used for
this diagnostic because they provide a direct model-based importance vector
over the shared feature set while the gradient-boosting AUC remains the primary
predictive-performance metric. The raw per-feature importances are
normalised to sum to one for each training source, giving an
importance-mass vector whose scale is comparable across sources. The
normalised profiles are inspected visually as side-by-side bars and are
summarised by a single agreement score based on the $L_1$
(total-variation) distance. Let $p^{\mathrm{real}}$ and $p^{g}$ denote the
normalised importance vectors of the real-trained reference and of
generator $g$ over the shared feature set $F$. The reported
feature-importance agreement is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FI}_g
  \;=\;
  1 - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{f\in F}\bigl| p^{g}_f - p^{\mathrm{real}}_f \bigr|,
  \label{eq:fi-l1}
\end{equation}
which is one minus the total-variation distance between the two
importance-mass vectors. Because both vectors are normalised, the sum lies
in $[0,2]$ and $\mathrm{FI}_g\in[0,1]$, with $\mathrm{FI}_g=1$ for
identical allocations and $\mathrm{FI}_g=0$ when the profiles place
importance on disjoint feature sets. The score therefore measures
preservation of feature-importance mass, not exact rank agreement. Because the random-forest importances used here are
mean-decrease-in-impurity (MDI) importances, they can be biased toward
variables with more available split points and can vary with the fitted
forest. The FI score is therefore interpreted as an interpretability-oriented
utility diagnostic rather than as a stand-alone ranking of
clinical importance. Variable-importance agreement has similarly been used
as a utility diagnostic in PATE-GAN and clinical synthetic-data
evaluations~\cite{pategan,beaulieu2019privacy}.

\paragraph{Cox Proportional-Hazards Agreement:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox}
Beyond binary classification, the readmission target is also
modelled as a time-to-event outcome through a Cox proportional
hazards regression~\cite{Cox1972PH}. The duration is the recorded
length of stay (\emph{time in hospital}) and the event is the
binary readmission indicator. The covariate set comprises thirteen
clinically meaningful columns and a small penaliser of $0.01$ is applied
to the partial likelihood to ensure convergence on columns with low
variance. The model is fitted separately on the real training table
and on each synthetic training table, and the per-covariate hazard
ratios with their $95\%$ confidence intervals are reported. These
ratios express \emph{the survival-style temporal-association
structure} between covariates and the readmission outcome, which is
not directly tested by any classification metric and which is of
direct clinical relevance for outcomes research on the Diabetes
130-US benchmark. Covariates with zero observed variance on a given
synthetic table, a failure mode that occurs when a generator
collapses an ordinal column onto a single value, are excluded
from that generator's fit and noted in the results chapter.
Cox-based reproduction of clinical analyses has also been used in
synthetic-health-data evaluations that compare real and synthetic
hazard-ratio estimates~\cite{healthgan,yale2020synthesizing}.

\paragraph{Cox Confidence-Interval Overlap:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox-overlap}
The available Cox hazard ratios for each generator are summarised
against the real-data Cox ratios by computing, for each shared covariate,
the overlap between the real-fit $95\%$ confidence interval and the
synthetic-fit $95\%$ confidence interval. The reported summary is
the fraction of covariates for which the two intervals overlap. A
generator that preserves the survival-style association structure
will produce hazard ratios whose confidence intervals overlap those
of the real fit, while a generator that distorts the structure will
produce non-overlapping intervals on the affected covariates. This
overlap fraction expresses \emph{agreement at the level of clinically
interpretable effect sizes} and is therefore a clinically interpretable
summary in the predictive branch.

\subsection{Privacy Metrics}
\label{sec:eval:privacy}

Privacy is operationalised through two complementary classes of
metric: instance-level distance metrics, which interrogate how
close in feature space any synthetic record is to any real record,
and inference-attack simulations, which directly enact a specific
adversarial protocol against the synthetic dataset. The two classes
are reported in parallel because each is blind to a failure mode
that the other detects. A generator can produce records that are
distant from any single real record yet still leak membership
through aggregate distance statistics, and conversely a generator
can resist a specific attack while leaving outliers vulnerable to
nearest-neighbour linkage. These metrics are empirical: they quantify
resistance to specific attacks and proximity patterns rather than
providing a formal guarantee. For the differentially private
generators, the $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ budget established in
\S\ref{sec:bg:dp} and summarised in \S\ref{sec:methods:summary} is the
privacy statement that these metrics corroborate. For the non-private
generators, where no such guarantee exists, they are the only available
privacy evidence. Before any privacy metric is computed,
every numeric column is $z$-score standardised so that the privacy
geometry is not dominated by columns with naturally large absolute
ranges.

\paragraph{Nearest-Neighbour Distance:}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:nn}
The most direct instance-level distance metric is the Euclidean
distance, in the standardised feature space, from each synthetic
record to its nearest real record (the \emph{synthetic-to-real
distance}, $d_{S\to R}$) and the symmetric distance from each real
record to its nearest synthetic record (the \emph{real-to-synthetic
distance}, $d_{R\to S}$). Median and fifth-percentile summaries of
both distributions are reported. The fifth percentile is the more 
privacy-relevant summary. It isolates the \emph{tail} of synthetic 
records closest to the real data. These are the records most likely 
to pose a re-identification hazard. In contrast, the median reflects 
typical records with little disclosure risk. This summary reflects
\emph{point-wise proximity}, a necessary precondition for several
distance-based linkage attacks documented in the synthetic-data
privacy literature~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,goncalves2020generation,wang2019generating}.
Real-to-real nearest-neighbour distances (using the second-nearest
neighbour to exclude self-matches) are computed once and form the
reference scale against which the synthetic-to-real distances are
read.

\paragraph{Nearest-Neighbour Distance Ratio (NNDR):}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:nndr}
The Nearest-Neighbour Distance Ratio, used in tabular synthetic-data
evaluation frameworks such as SynthEval~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval},
normalises synthetic-to-real proximity against the intrinsic packing
density of the real distribution. For each synthetic record, its
synthetic-to-real distance $d_{S\to R}$ is divided by a real-to-real
nearest-neighbour distance drawn uniformly at random from the real
table (computed once, using the second-nearest neighbour to exclude
self-matches), with a small constant added to the denominator to avoid
division by zero on degenerate real records. The median of these
per-record ratios is reported per generator. This formulation differs
from the SynthEval variant, which divides the distance to the nearest
real record by the distance to the second-nearest; the randomly drawn
real-to-real denominator used here instead reads each synthetic record's
proximity against the real data's own spacing.
Values $\mathrm{NNDR} \approx 1$ indicate that synthetic records lie 
at distances similar to the natural spacing between real records. 
This is treated as the privacy-neutral regime. Values $\mathrm{NNDR} < 1$ 
indicate that synthetic records are closer to real records than real 
records are to each other. This suggests possible memorisation. 
Values $\mathrm{NNDR} > 1$ indicate that synthetic records are 
systematically farther from real records than real records are from 
each other. This corresponds to the conservative privacy regime.
As with $\mathrm{AA} > 0.5$ below, $\mathrm{NNDR} > 1$
is not by itself evidence of good privacy: it can equally reflect a
synthetic distribution that has drifted away from the real data, so it
is read jointly with the utility metrics rather than in isolation. This
ratio expresses \emph{scale-normalised proximity}, a dimension the raw
distance statistics of the previous paragraph cannot capture.

\paragraph{Nearest-Neighbour Adversarial Accuracy (NNAA):}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:nnaa}
The Nearest-Neighbour Adversarial Accuracy, introduced by \textcite{healthgan},
is a single-number summary of how indistinguishable real and synthetic
records are to a nearest-neighbour adversary. Let $T$ and $S$ denote a
real and a synthetic record set, each of size $n$; the adversarial
accuracy (abbreviated $\mathrm{AA}$) is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{AA}_{TS}
  \;=\;
  \frac{1}{2}\Biggl(
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}\bigl(d_{TS}(i) > d_{TT}(i)\bigr)
    \;+\;
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}\bigl(d_{ST}(i) > d_{SS}(i)\bigr)
  \Biggr),
  \label{eq:nnaa}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function, $d_{TS}(i)$ is the
nearest-neighbour distance from the $i$-th record of $T$ to the set $S$,
and $d_{TT}(i)$ is the distance from that record to its nearest
\emph{other} record within $T$; $d_{ST}(i)$ and $d_{SS}(i)$ are
symmetric for the $i$-th record of $S$. Each sum is thus the fraction
of records lying farther from the opposite set than from their own
nearest neighbour. The target is $\mathrm{AA} \approx 0.5$, at which the
adversary cannot decide whether a query point is real or synthetic;
$\mathrm{AA} < 0.5$ signals memorisation (synthetic records closer to
real records than real records are to each other), while
$\mathrm{AA} > 0.5$ signals a distributional shift (synthetic records
clustering away from the real data). Following the train/test setup of
\textcite{healthgan}, $\mathrm{AA}$ is computed against both a training
and a test subsample from an $80/20$ split of the real data, and the
memorisation gap
$\mathrm{PrivacyLoss} = \mathrm{AA}_{\mathrm{test}} - \mathrm{AA}_{\mathrm{train}}$
is positive when a generator sits closer to the records it was trained
on than to unseen ones.

\paragraph{Membership Inference Attack:}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:mia}
A membership inference adversary~\cite{Shokri2017MIA} attempts to
decide, given a candidate record and access to the synthetic
dataset, whether the candidate was in the generator's training set.
The simulated adversary proceeds as follows. For each
candidate record, the distances to the first, third, and fifth
nearest synthetic neighbours, together with their mean, are computed
in the standardised feature space and stacked into a four-component
feature vector. The training (member) and test (non-member) splits
of the real data supply the positive and negative labels, with
sample sizes balanced to a fixed cap. A logistic-regression
adversary is then fit on the resulting feature-label pairs under
five-fold cross-validation, and the cross-validated probability of
membership is used to compute the receiver-operating-characteristic
area $\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{MIA}}$. The reported summary is the
attacker's \emph{advantage} over a random guess,
$\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{MIA}} = \mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{MIA}} - 0.5$,
which equals zero when the adversary cannot distinguish members from
non-members and increases as the synthetic distribution leaks
training-set information. Membership-inference risk is also catalogued
in synthetic-data surveys~\cite{Murtaza2023,hernandez2022synthetic}.
In this implementation, the advantage thresholds $\{0.05, 0.10\}$ are
used as descriptive bins for low, medium, and high privacy risk. This
attack measures \emph{aggregate distance-pattern leakage} of the
training set into the synthetic distribution, and is complementary to
the per-record proximity metrics above because it pools information
across multiple synthetic neighbours and across all candidate records
rather than evaluating any single record's risk in isolation.

\paragraph{Attribute Inference Attack:}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:aia}
An attribute inference adversary attempts to infer a sensitive
attribute of a real record given the rest of that record and access
to the synthetic dataset. The protocol simulated here follows the
nearest-neighbour formulation discussed in the survey of
\textcite{Murtaza2023}. For each of four sensitive attributes
(readmission status, gender, age, and primary diagnosis category),
the remaining columns are taken as known features, every real
record is matched to its ten nearest synthetic neighbours in the
standardised known-feature space, and the value of the sensitive
attribute is predicted by majority vote among those neighbours. The
inference accuracy is compared against a trivial majority-class
baseline; the reported summary is the
\emph{adversary advantage}
$\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{AIA}} = \mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{AIA}}
- \mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{majority}}$, which equals zero when the
adversary does no better than always predicting the most common
value and increases when the synthetic dataset encodes information
about the sensitive attribute conditional on the other features.
This attack measures \emph{conditional information leakage} on a
specific sensitive column, and is the privacy counterpart to the
TSTR utility metric: TSTR rewards a generator for preserving the
conditional distribution that makes the sensitive attribute
predictable, while attribute inference flags the same preservation
as a disclosure risk if it permits a third party to reconstruct the
attribute from the rest of the record.

\paragraph{Clustering Proximity and Outlier Linkability:}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:linkability}
Outliers, patients whose feature vector sits far from the bulk
of the real distribution, are at disproportionate
re-identification risk in synthetic data, as emphasised by
\textcite{stadler2022synthetic} and \textcite{wang2019generating}.
The linkability metric isolates this regime as follows. In the 
standardised real feature space, real records are ranked by Euclidean 
distance from the real centroid. The top five per cent are declared 
outliers. An equal-sized random sample of non-outlier records is then 
drawn as a contrast group. A radius $\rho$ is set equal
to the seventy-fifth percentile of the real-to-real
nearest-neighbour distances, which keeps the radius large enough
that even sparse outlier regions can contain synthetic neighbours
in the conservative privacy regime. For each generator, the
fraction of outliers with at least one synthetic record within
radius $\rho$ is computed (the \emph{outlier linkability}), together
with the same fraction on the non-outlier contrast sample (the
\emph{non-outlier linkability}). The gap between them is reported as
a third single number. This gap exposes \emph{differential
disclosure risk for outliers}, which the aggregate distance and
inference metrics above can mask: a generator can score well on
median NNDR and on aggregate MIA while still placing synthetic
neighbours close to a small number of outlier real records.

\subsection{Comparative Aggregation}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate}

The per-pillar metrics defined in \S\ref{sec:eval:utility} and
\S\ref{sec:eval:privacy} are combined for RQ2 and RQ3 through a small
set of aggregation views. These views are interpretive rather than
decisive: the comparative claims remain grounded in the per-metric
values, not in a single composite score, following the multi-axis
reporting logic of SynthEval~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}. The
aggregation proceeds from raw to scaled views. A comprehensive table
preserves the unscaled metric values; a privacy-utility landscape uses
Gradient-Boosting TSTR AUC-ROC and median NNDR as representative utility
and privacy axes; and scaled visual summaries expose within-pillar
structure. Pareto dominance is assessed on the raw AUC-ROC/NNDR plane,
so the main balance claim does not depend on an averaged score. This
joint privacy-utility framing follows synthetic-data evaluation
surveys~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023}, and the visual
risk-utility presentation follows \textcite{thees2025beyond}.

\paragraph{Comprehensive Summary Table:}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:table}
The headline numerical summary is a single table whose twelve rows are
the headline single-number metrics defined in \S\ref{sec:eval:utility} and
\S\ref{sec:eval:privacy}, with six columns (the real-data TRTR
reference plus the five generators), grouped by pillar and branch
for readability. The table preserves the raw metric values, preferred
directions, and pillar grouping. It therefore serves as the numerical
reference for the visual comparisons that follow, without introducing an
additional aggregation or ranking rule. It also functions as the input
for the empirical diagnostic-power analysis of RQ1
(\S\ref{sec:results:rq1:diagnostic}), in which the spread of each
metric across the generator columns is examined to decide which
metrics actually separate the generators and which collapse to a
narrow range.

\paragraph{Privacy-Utility Landscape:}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:tradeoff}
The landscape view plots the median NNDR on the privacy axis against the
gradient-boosting TSTR AUC-ROC on the utility axis. It also places each generator
at a single point and uses the TRTR baseline as a reference. This view
identifies the Pareto frontier, i.e.\ the generators not strictly
dominated on both axes, and provides the main two-dimensional comparison
for RQ2 (\S\ref{sec:results:rq2:landscape}). The within-family
differential-privacy transitions analysed for RQ3 are shown on the same
plane as directional arrows (\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:arrow-plot}). Median
NNDR is used as the privacy axis because it provides a scale-normalised
instance-level distance measure with sufficient variation for an
interpretable two-dimensional display. The attack-based privacy metrics,
namely membership inference, attribute inference, and outlier linkability,
remain reported in the comprehensive summary table so that the axis choice
does not conceal residual leakage. The risk-utility map presentation
follows \textcite{thees2025beyond}.


\paragraph{Utility Radar:}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:radar}
The radar chart provides a scaled profile of the utility pillar after the
two-dimensional landscape has reduced utility to a single predictive axis.
It displays statistical similarity, predictive performance, Cox
confidence-interval overlap, and feature-importance agreement on a common
$[0,1]$ scale, with larger values indicating better performance. The chart
is restricted to utility metrics because these measures can be oriented in
a shared favourable direction. A separate privacy radar is not used because 
several privacy metrics have
threshold or two-sided interpretations rather than a single monotonic
``larger is better'' direction, which would make the radial profile
misleading. Privacy metrics are instead handled in the
separate heatmap, where their threshold-dependent interpretation can be
shown more explicitly. The radar is used as a qualitative profile view
rather than as a scalar ranking of generators. The use of bounded,
directionally aligned axes for radar-style visualisation follows
\textcite{thees2025beyond}.

\paragraph{Utility-Privacy Heatmap:}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:heatmap}
The heatmap provides the metric-level inspection view. Each metric is
oriented and min-max scaled to $[0,1]$ across the generators before
display. Utility metrics are oriented so that larger values indicate
better performance. Privacy metrics are expressed as risk-oriented
quantities, so that larger values indicate greater disclosure concern.
For NNDR, the risk contribution is defined as
$\max(0,\,1-\mathrm{NNDR})$. For nearest-neighbour adversarial accuracy
and membership-inference AUC, only deviation on the leakage side of the
$0.5$ reference value is counted, using
$\max(0,\,1-2\,\mathrm{AA})$ and $\max(0,\,2\,\mathrm{AUC}-1)$,
respectively. Attribute-inference advantage, outlier linkability, and
positive privacy loss are already non-negative risk quantities.

This one-sided orientation is a thesis-specific design choice: it treats
near-record leakage as a privacy risk, while treating excessive distance
from the real distribution as a utility or fidelity failure rather than
as a privacy failure. This is appropriate for the present comparison
because off-manifold behaviour is already captured by the utility metrics,
but it also means that a collapsed generator can appear low-risk in the
privacy block unless the heatmap is read together with the raw summary
table and the privacy-utility landscape. The heatmap therefore shows the
full metric pattern without collapsing the metrics into a composite mean.
The general use of scaled heatmaps for multivariate risk-utility
inspection follows \textcite{thees2025beyond}.
