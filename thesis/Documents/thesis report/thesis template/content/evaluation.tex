\subsection{Two Pillars: Utility and Privacy}
\label{sec:eval:pillars}

This thesis adopts the two-pillar evaluation structure recommended by
the synthetic-data surveys of \textcite{hernandez2022synthetic} and
\textcite{Murtaza2023}: every generator is assessed along a
\emph{utility} axis and a \emph{privacy} axis, with the two reported
separately so that trade-offs between them can be made explicit. The
utility pillar is itself decomposed into two complementary branches.
The first, \emph{statistical similarity}
(\S\ref{sec:eval:utility:similarity}), asks whether the synthetic
table reproduces the distributional shape of the real data --- both
the per-column marginals and the joint structure across columns.
The second, \emph{predictive performance}
(\S\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive}), asks whether the synthetic
table behaves like the real one when used to train downstream
predictive and survival models. The privacy pillar
(\S\ref{sec:eval:privacy}) is operationalised through instance-level
distance metrics and explicit inference-attack simulations.
Section~\ref{sec:eval:aggregate} introduces the two aggregation views
through which the pillars are combined into a single comparative
picture for RQ2 and RQ3.

Two design principles govern the choice of metrics throughout this
chapter. First, the metrics within each branch are deliberately
chosen to be \emph{complementary} in what they measure --- a marginal
metric, a joint-structure metric, and a manifold-geometry metric are
all reported in the similarity branch, for instance, because a
generator that scores well on one need not score well on another and
the empirical answer to RQ1 depends on this complementarity. Second,
every metric is reported using the same numerical conventions across
generators: the same train/test split, the same standardisation
choices, the same downstream classifiers, and the same Laplace,
clipping, and noise calibrations. This uniformity is what makes the
cross-generator comparisons in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} sound. The
empirical question of which metrics actually \emph{discriminate}
between the five generators on the Diabetes 130-US Hospitals benchmark
--- and which collapse to a single value --- is addressed in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq1:diagnostic}. The metrics adopted here overlap
substantially with those of established evaluation frameworks such as SynthEval,
which likewise reports complementary utility and privacy metrics and ranks
generators per metric rather than collapsing them into a single composite
score~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}; this convergence supports the selection used
in this thesis.

\subsection{Utility Metrics}
\label{sec:eval:utility}

The utility pillar combines two complementary aspects. The first is
whether the synthetic dataset \emph{looks like} the real one in a
distributional sense; the second is whether it \emph{acts like} the
real one when used as training data for downstream models. The same
synthetic table is evaluated under both views, so that, for example, a
generator that achieves close marginal fits but fails to support
predictive learning can be diagnosed as preserving univariate but not
conditional structure.

\subsubsection{Statistical Similarity to the Real Data}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity}

\paragraph{Per-Column Distributional Match.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:dist}
The first diagnostic is purely visual: for each column the empirical
density of the real and the synthetic values is overlaid on the same
axis, using kernel-density estimates for continuous variables and
side-by-side bar charts for categorical and binary variables. The
purpose of this diagnostic is to expose obvious failure modes that
single-number summaries hide, such as mode collapse on a dominant
category, distributional bimodality flattened into unimodality, or a
synthetic distribution placed on a range disjoint from the real
support. Following the practice surveyed by
\textcite{hernandez2022synthetic} and \textcite{Murtaza2023}, no
single-number summary is attached to these plots; they are reported in
the results chapter (\S\ref{sec:results:rq2:utility:similarity}) and
in an appendix gallery so that any anomaly visible in the
single-number metrics that follow can be cross-checked at the
column level.

\paragraph{Summary Statistics (Mean, Standard Deviation).}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:stats}
The simplest single-number per-column summaries used here are the
sample mean and sample standard deviation of every column, computed
separately on the real and on each synthetic table. The pair
$(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{real}}, \hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{real}})$ versus
$(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{synth}}, \hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{synth}})$ is
informative even though it ignores higher moments and the entire
joint distribution: large discrepancies in either statistic indicate
that the generator has placed mass on values that do not match the
column's central tendency or spread, which is a much stronger failure
than a per-shape mismatch that preserves the first two moments. This
metric captures the \emph{first-moment fidelity} dimension of the
similarity branch and is blind to all dependencies between columns and
to any shape information beyond the first two moments. Columns whose
real-data mean and standard deviation are both zero (degenerate
columns introduced by the medication binarisation step in
\S\ref{sec:data:fe}) are excluded from the comparison so
that ratios remain defined.

\paragraph{Pairwise Correlation Difference (PCD).}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:pcd}
A single-number summary of the joint linear structure across columns
is the Pairwise Correlation Difference introduced by
\textcite{goncalves2020generation} for synthetic patient data. Let
$C^{\mathrm{real}}$ and $C^{\mathrm{synth}}$ denote the Pearson
correlation matrices of the real and synthetic tables computed over
the same column set; the PCD is the mean absolute entry of their
difference, restricted here to the strictly lower triangle to avoid
double-counting symmetric pairs:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PCD}(C^{\mathrm{real}}, C^{\mathrm{synth}})
  \;=\;
  \frac{1}{|L|}
  \sum_{(i,j)\in L} \bigl| C^{\mathrm{real}}_{ij} - C^{\mathrm{synth}}_{ij} \bigr|,
  \qquad
  L = \{(i,j) : i > j\}.
  \label{eq:pcd}
\end{equation}
Smaller values indicate closer agreement of the joint linear
structure. PCD is designed to capture \emph{joint linear dependency
fidelity} --- the dimension of similarity that the per-column metrics
above are blind to --- and is itself blind to non-linear dependency,
to higher-order multivariate structure, and to univariate shape
distortions that leave the linear-dependence pattern intact.
Following \textcite{goncalves2020generation}, the per-pair entries of
$|C^{\mathrm{real}} - C^{\mathrm{synth}}|$ are also reported as
heatmaps, both because the heatmap localises which column pairs the
generator failed to model and because the heatmap exposes the
correlation-sign-flip failure mode (a generator that inverts the sign
of a real correlation produces a large per-pair contribution that the
scalar PCD averages away).

\paragraph{Kolmogorov--Smirnov Two-Sample Test.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:ks}
The Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) two-sample test is a non-parametric
distance between the empirical cumulative distribution functions of
two samples. For a single column with real and synthetic empirical
CDFs $F^{\mathrm{real}}$ and $F^{\mathrm{synth}}$, the KS statistic is
\begin{equation}
  D_{KS}
  \;=\;
  \sup_{x} \bigl| F^{\mathrm{real}}(x) - F^{\mathrm{synth}}(x) \bigr|,
  \label{eq:ks}
\end{equation}
which takes values in $[0, 1]$ and equals zero when the two empirical
distributions coincide. The two-sample KS statistic is reported
per column and averaged across columns to summarise marginal fidelity
in a single number. The mean KS captures \emph{worst-case marginal
discrepancy} aggregated across columns and is more sensitive to
distribution-shape errors than the moment-based summaries of the
previous paragraph; it is, however, computed independently per column
and therefore conveys no information about joint structure. The
medication-flag columns are excluded from the aggregation because
their values are concentrated on a single category, making the KS
statistic numerically unstable. KS is the canonical univariate
distance reported in the synthetic-medical-data surveys of
\textcite{Murtaza2023} and \textcite{goncalves2020generation}.

\paragraph{PCA-Based Manifold Overlap.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:pca}
A two-dimensional principal-component projection of the real and the
synthetic tables onto a shared coordinate system is used as a visual
diagnostic of overall manifold geometry. All columns are first
$z$-score standardised on the combined real-plus-synthetic table so
that no single column dominates the variance decomposition; principal
components are then estimated on the combined table, and every
record --- real or synthetic --- is projected onto the first two
components and plotted with a generator-specific colour. To keep the
scatter readable on a single page, both the real sample and each
synthetic sample are subsampled to at most $5{,}000$ records before
projection. This diagnostic captures the \emph{whole-distribution
geometric overlap} dimension that the per-column and pairwise metrics
do not address: a generator can score reasonably on KS and PCD while
nevertheless concentrating its mass in a region of the principal-axes
plane that the real data does not occupy. Conversely, a wide overlap
on the PCA plot is necessary but not sufficient for distributional
fidelity, so this metric is treated as a visual cross-check rather
than as a primary summary statistic.

\subsubsection{Predictive Performance of Downstream Models}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive}

\paragraph{Train--Test Regimes: TRTR and TSTR.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:tstr}
Predictive utility is operationalised through the Train-on-Synthetic,
Test-on-Real (TSTR) protocol introduced by~\textcite{Esteban2017TSTR}
and adopted as the standard utility measure in the tabular-synthetic
survey of \textcite{hernandez2022synthetic}. The protocol fits a
classifier on the synthetic table, evaluates it on the held-out real
test split, and compares the resulting performance to a Train-on-Real,
Test-on-Real (TRTR) baseline in which the same classifier is trained
on the real training split and evaluated on the same real test split.
The TRTR baseline is the upper bound that any generator can realistically
hope to recover: a generator that supports downstream learning as well
as the real data itself reaches the TRTR score, and the gap between
TSTR and TRTR is the natural measure of utility loss attributable to
synthesis. This protocol captures the \emph{transferability of
conditional structure} from the synthetic table to a downstream task
--- precisely the dimension that a per-column statistical-similarity
metric cannot expose --- and is the operational expression of the
common claim that a synthetic dataset must \emph{act like} the real one
rather than merely \emph{look like} it. The dual regimes TRTS (Train on
Real, Test on Synthetic) and TSTS (Train on Synthetic, Test on
Synthetic) are computed alongside TSTR for completeness; TRTS in
particular is informative because a generator that produces synthetic
test examples that the TRTR-fitted classifier handles well shows that
synthetic and real distributions agree at the decision boundary, while
the symmetric gap TSTR$-$TRTS exposes train-side versus test-side
shifts in the synthetic distribution.

\paragraph{Classifier Suite.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:classifiers}
Three downstream classifiers are evaluated in parallel, chosen to
span linear, ensemble-tree, and gradient-boosted regimes:
logistic regression with default $\ell_2$ regularisation and up to
$1{,}000$ optimisation iterations; a random forest with $100$ trees
under default split settings; and gradient boosting with $100$ boosting
rounds. Reporting all three rather than a single ``best'' classifier
limits the risk that a generator's utility ranking is an artefact of
the particular inductive bias of one classifier --- a concern raised
in the synthetic-data survey of \textcite{Murtaza2023}. Before
classifier fitting, missing values in any training source are imputed
by per-column median imputation fit on the training partition and
applied to the test partition; this guards against information leakage
from test statistics into the imputation. All classifiers are seeded
with a fixed random state so that the cross-generator comparison is
not perturbed by run-to-run variance within a classifier.

\paragraph{Performance Metrics.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:metrics}
For each (Train Source, Classifier) cell five metrics are reported:
classification accuracy, macro-F1, the area under the
receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), precision, and
recall, with the positive class defined as the readmission indicator.
The full five-metric set is retained because accuracy alone is
misleading on the moderately imbalanced readmission target; F1 and
the precision/recall pair expose threshold-dependent failure modes
that AUC-ROC averages out, and AUC-ROC in turn captures
threshold-independent ranking quality that F1 ignores. Accuracy
captures \emph{aggregate calibration of the decision rule}; F1
captures \emph{balance between false positives and false negatives at
the chosen threshold}; AUC-ROC captures \emph{threshold-independent
ranking quality}. A generator can score well on one of these and
poorly on the others, so they are treated as complementary rather
than as alternative scalarisations of the same underlying quantity.

\paragraph{ROC Curve Analysis.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:roc}
The gradient-boosting classifier is additionally used to generate
ROC-curve overlays for the TRTR baseline and each TSTR generator.
The overlay diagnoses whether a TSTR generator's gap to the baseline
is concentrated at a particular operating point along the curve or
spread uniformly across thresholds, which is information that the
scalar AUC-ROC of the previous paragraph collapses away.

\paragraph{Feature Importance Preservation.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:fi}
A complementary check on conditional structure is whether the
random-forest variable-importance ranking obtained from a
synthetic-trained classifier matches the ranking obtained from the
real-trained classifier. The comparison is summarised by the Spearman
rank correlation between the two importance vectors, on the principle
that two classifiers trained on substantively different data
distributions will assign substantively different rankings even when
their aggregate predictive performance happens to coincide. This
metric captures \emph{the locus of predictive signal} rather than its
magnitude, and is therefore complementary to AUC-ROC: a high
Spearman rank correlation indicates that synthetic and real data
agree on which features are doing the predictive work, while a low
rank correlation indicates that the synthetic data has shifted the
signal onto a different feature set.

\paragraph{Cox Proportional Hazards Regression.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox}
Beyond binary classification, the readmission target is also
modelled as a time-to-event outcome through a Cox proportional
hazards regression~\cite{Cox1972PH}. The duration is the recorded
length of stay (\emph{time in hospital}) and the event is the
binary readmission indicator. The covariate set comprises thirteen
clinically meaningful columns: patient age, gender, admission type,
medication and laboratory counts, the number of inpatient,
emergency, and outpatient visits, the diagnosis counts, the insulin
prescription indicator, the diabetes-medication indicator, and the
medication-change indicator. A small penaliser of $0.01$ is applied
to the partial likelihood to ensure convergence on columns with low
variance. The model is fitted separately on the real training table
and on each synthetic training table, and the per-covariate hazard
ratios with their $95\%$ confidence intervals are reported. This
metric captures \emph{the survival-style temporal-association
structure} between covariates and the readmission outcome, which is
not directly tested by any classification metric and which is of
direct clinical relevance for outcomes research on the Diabetes
130-US benchmark. Covariates with zero observed variance on a given
synthetic table --- a failure mode that occurs when a generator
collapses an ordinal column onto a single value --- are excluded
from that generator's fit and noted in the results chapter.

\paragraph{Cox Confidence-Interval Overlap.}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox-overlap}
The seven Cox hazard ratios obtained per generator are summarised
against the real-data Cox ratios by computing, for each covariate,
the overlap between the real-fit $95\%$ confidence interval and the
synthetic-fit $95\%$ confidence interval. The reported summary is
the fraction of covariates for which the two intervals overlap; a
generator that preserves the survival-style association structure
will produce hazard ratios whose confidence intervals overlap those
of the real fit, while a generator that distorts the structure will
produce non-overlapping intervals on the affected covariates. The
metric captures \emph{agreement at the level of clinically
interpretable effect sizes} and is therefore the most directly
clinically actionable summary in the predictive branch.

\subsection{Privacy Metrics}
\label{sec:eval:privacy}

Privacy is operationalised through two complementary classes of
metric: instance-level distance metrics, which interrogate how
close in feature space any synthetic record is to any real record,
and inference-attack simulations, which directly enact a specific
adversarial protocol against the synthetic dataset. The two classes
are reported in parallel because each is blind to a failure mode
that the other detects: a generator can produce records that are
distant from any single real record yet still leak membership
through aggregate distance statistics, and conversely a generator
can resist a specific attack while leaving outliers vulnerable to
nearest-neighbour linkage. Before any privacy metric is computed,
every numeric column is $z$-score standardised so that the privacy
geometry is not dominated by columns with naturally large absolute
ranges.

\paragraph{Nearest-Neighbour Distance.}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:nn}
The most direct instance-level distance metric is the Euclidean
distance, in the standardised feature space, from each synthetic
record to its nearest real record (the \emph{synthetic-to-real
distance}, $d_{S\to R}$) and the symmetric distance from each real
record to its nearest synthetic record (the \emph{real-to-synthetic
distance}, $d_{R\to S}$). Median and fifth-percentile summaries of
both distributions are reported. The fifth percentile is the more
privacy-relevant of the two summaries because it isolates the
\emph{tail} of synthetic records that are closest to the real data
--- the records most likely to constitute a re-identification
hazard --- whereas the median characterises the bulk of the
synthetic distribution and is dominated by typical records that
carry little disclosure risk. This metric captures
\emph{point-wise proximity}, a necessary precondition for several
distance-based linkage attacks documented in the synthetic-data
privacy literature~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,goncalves2020generation,wang2019generating}.
Real-to-real nearest-neighbour distances (using the second-nearest
neighbour to exclude self-matches) are computed once and form the
reference scale against which the synthetic-to-real distances are
read.

\paragraph{Nearest-Neighbour Distance Ratio (NNDR).}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:nndr}
The Nearest-Neighbour Distance Ratio normalises synthetic-to-real
proximity against the intrinsic packing density of the real
distribution. Let $d^{(j)}_{S\to R}$ denote the synthetic-to-real
distance for synthetic record $j$, and let $r_i$ denote the
real-to-real nearest-neighbour distance for real record $i$
(computed once over the real table, using the second-nearest
neighbour to exclude self-matches). The implementation reported here
pairs each synthetic record with a randomly drawn real-to-real
reference distance and forms the ratio
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{NNDR}^{(j)} \;=\; \frac{d^{(j)}_{S\to R}}{r_{\pi(j)} + \varepsilon},
\end{equation}
where $\pi$ is a uniform random pairing over the real records and
$\varepsilon$ is a small constant to avoid division by zero on
degenerate real records. Median and mean NNDR are reported per
generator. Values $\mathrm{NNDR} \approx 1$ indicate that synthetic
records sit at distances commensurate with the intrinsic spacing of
real records (the privacy-neutral regime), values
$\mathrm{NNDR} < 1$ indicate that the synthetic distribution is
concentrated closer to real records than the real distribution is to
itself (a memorisation signal), and values $\mathrm{NNDR} > 1$
indicate that synthetic records are systematically further from real
records than real records are from each other (the conservative
privacy regime). This metric captures \emph{the scale-normalised
proximity} dimension that the raw distance statistics of the
previous paragraph alone cannot express, because a small absolute
distance is privacy-meaningful only relative to the natural spread
of the real data.

\paragraph{Nearest-Neighbour Adversarial Accuracy (NNAA).}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:nnaa}
\textcite{yale2020synthesizing} introduce the Nearest-Neighbour
Adversarial Accuracy as a single-number summary that quantifies the
indistinguishability of real and synthetic records by a
nearest-neighbour adversary. Let $T$ and $S$ denote a real and a
synthetic record set, respectively; the adversarial accuracy is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{AA}(T, S)
  \;=\;
  \tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(
    \Pr\bigl[d_{T \to S} > d_{T \to T}\bigr]
    \;+\;
    \Pr\bigl[d_{S \to T} > d_{S \to S}\bigr]
  \Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $d_{T \to S}$ is the nearest-neighbour distance from a record
of $T$ to the set $S$, $d_{T \to T}$ is the nearest-neighbour
distance from a record of $T$ to the rest of $T$ (excluding the
record itself), and analogously for $d_{S\to T}$ and $d_{S\to S}$.
The target value is $\mathrm{AA} \approx 0.5$, at which a
nearest-neighbour adversary cannot decide whether a query point came
from the real or the synthetic distribution; values $\mathrm{AA} < 0.5$
indicate that synthetic records are closer to real records than real
records are to each other (a memorisation signal), and values
$\mathrm{AA} > 0.5$ indicate that synthetic records cluster among
themselves and away from real records (a distributional-shift
signal). Following \textcite{yale2020synthesizing}, $\mathrm{AA}$ is
reported on both a real training subsample and a real test subsample
obtained from an $80/20$ split of the real data, and the difference
$\mathrm{PrivacyLoss} = \mathrm{AA}_{\mathrm{test}} -
\mathrm{AA}_{\mathrm{train}}$ is reported as a memorisation
diagnostic: a generator that has memorised the training subsample
will produce $\mathrm{AA}_{\mathrm{train}} < \mathrm{AA}_{\mathrm{test}}$
because synthetic records are closer to the records they were trained
on than to records they have never seen, giving a positive privacy
loss. This metric captures \emph{the indistinguishability of synthetic
records from real records under a nearest-neighbour adversary}, which
is conceptually different from the raw distance statistics above
because it is normalised against the real distribution's own
nearest-neighbour structure on both sides of the comparison.

\paragraph{Membership Inference Attack.}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:mia}
A membership inference adversary~\cite{Shokri2017MIA} attempts to
decide, given a candidate record and access to the synthetic
dataset, whether the candidate was in the generator's training set.
The simulated adversary used here proceeds as follows. For each
candidate record, the distances to the first, third, and fifth
nearest synthetic neighbours, together with their mean, are computed
in the standardised feature space and stacked into a four-component
feature vector. The training (member) and test (non-member) splits
of the real data supply the positive and negative labels, with
sample sizes balanced to a fixed cap. A logistic-regression
adversary is then fit on the resulting feature--label pairs under
five-fold cross-validation, and the cross-validated probability of
membership is used to compute the receiver-operating-characteristic
area $\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{MIA}}$. The reported summary is the
attacker's \emph{advantage} over a random guess,
$\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{MIA}} = \mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{MIA}} - 0.5$,
which equals zero when the adversary cannot distinguish members from
non-members and increases as the synthetic distribution leaks
training-set information. The advantage thresholds
$\{0.05, 0.10\}$ are used to bin the result into low, medium, and
high privacy risk, following the conventions adopted by the
synthetic-data privacy surveys of
\textcite{Murtaza2023} and \textcite{hernandez2022synthetic}. This
metric captures \emph{aggregate distance-pattern leakage} of the
training set into the synthetic distribution, and is complementary to
the per-record proximity metrics above because it pools information
across multiple synthetic neighbours and across all candidate records
rather than evaluating any single record's risk in isolation.

\paragraph{Attribute Inference Attack.}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:aia}
An attribute inference adversary attempts to infer a sensitive
attribute of a real record given the rest of that record and access
to the synthetic dataset. The protocol simulated here follows the
nearest-neighbour formulation discussed in the survey of
\textcite{Murtaza2023}. For each of four sensitive attributes ---
readmission status, gender, age, and primary diagnosis category ---
the remaining columns are taken as known features, every real
record is matched to its ten nearest synthetic neighbours in the
standardised known-feature space, and the value of the sensitive
attribute is predicted by majority vote among those neighbours. The
inference accuracy is compared against a trivial majority-class
baseline; the reported summary is the
\emph{adversary advantage}
$\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{AIA}} = \mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{AIA}}
- \mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{majority}}$, which equals zero when the
adversary does no better than always predicting the most common
value and increases when the synthetic dataset encodes information
about the sensitive attribute conditional on the other features.
This metric captures \emph{conditional information leakage} on a
specific sensitive column, and is the privacy counterpart to the
TSTR utility metric: TSTR rewards a generator for preserving the
conditional distribution that makes the sensitive attribute
predictable, while attribute inference flags the same preservation
as a disclosure risk if it permits a third party to reconstruct the
attribute from the rest of the record.

\paragraph{Clustering Proximity and Outlier Linkability.}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:linkability}
Outliers --- patients whose feature vector sits far from the bulk
of the real distribution --- are at disproportionate
re-identification risk in synthetic data, as emphasised by
\textcite{stadler2022synthetic} and \textcite{wang2019generating}.
The metric reported here isolates this regime as follows. In the
standardised real feature space, the top five per cent of real
records ranked by Euclidean distance from the real centroid are
declared outliers, and an equal-sized random sample of non-outlier
records is drawn as a contrast group. A radius $\rho$ is set equal
to the seventy-fifth percentile of the real-to-real
nearest-neighbour distances, which keeps the radius large enough
that even sparse outlier regions can contain synthetic neighbours
in the conservative privacy regime. For each generator, the
fraction of outliers with at least one synthetic record within
radius $\rho$ is computed (the \emph{outlier linkability}), together
with the same fraction on the non-outlier contrast sample (the
\emph{non-outlier linkability}); the gap between them is reported as
a third single number. This metric captures \emph{differential
disclosure risk for outliers}, which the aggregate distance and
inference metrics above can mask: a generator can score well on
median NNDR and on aggregate MIA while still placing synthetic
neighbours close to a small number of outlier real records.

\subsection{Comparative Aggregation}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate}

Two aggregation views are used to combine the per-pillar metrics
into a single comparative picture. The \emph{landscape view}
positions every generator on a single privacy--utility plane and is
the main figure of RQ2 (\S\ref{sec:results:rq2:landscape}); the
\emph{within-family DP-toggle view} draws arrows between the
non-private and the differentially private variant of each family on
the same plane and is the main figure of RQ3
(\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:arrow-plot}). The choice of axes follows
directly from the metric design above: the utility axis uses the
gradient-boosting TSTR AUC-ROC as a single-number representative of
the predictive branch, and the privacy axis uses the median NNDR as
a single-number representative of the instance-level privacy
metrics. Both single-number axes are chosen for visual readability;
the multi-metric perspectives below mitigate the loss of detail
incurred by collapsing each pillar to one axis.

\paragraph{Privacy--Utility Trade-off Scatter.}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:tradeoff}
A scatter plot of median NNDR (privacy axis, larger is better)
against gradient-boosting TSTR AUC-ROC (utility axis, larger is
better) places every generator at a single point and adds the TRTR
baseline as an upper-right reference; the resulting visualisation
isolates the Pareto frontier and exposes any generator that is
strictly dominated on both axes by another.

\paragraph{Six-Dimension Radar Chart.}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:radar}
The trade-off plot above collapses each pillar to one axis at the
cost of suppressing within-pillar detail. A radar chart with six
axes restores that detail: three utility axes (statistical
similarity, predictive performance, Cox confidence-interval overlap)
and three privacy axes (nearest-neighbour distance, membership
inference, attribute inference). Each axis is rescaled to a
$[0, 1]$ interval with higher always indicating better, so that the
area enclosed by a generator's polygon can be read directly. The
radar chart is intended for qualitative shape comparison rather
than for ordering generators on a single scalar; it makes the
trade-off geometry visible at a glance.

\paragraph{Comprehensive Summary Table.}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:table}
The headline numerical summary is a single table with eleven rows
(one per single-number metric defined in \S\ref{sec:eval:utility} and
\S\ref{sec:eval:privacy}) and six columns (the real-data TRTR
reference plus the five generators), grouped by pillar and branch
for readability. The best-performing generator entry per row is
highlighted, with the TRTR reference excluded from the highlighting.
This table is the most information-dense view in the thesis and is
the artefact most likely to be consulted by readers seeking the raw
numbers behind the higher-level figures above; it also functions as
the input for the empirical diagnostic-power analysis of RQ1
