\section{Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:method:evaluation}

This section operationalises the utility and privacy concepts introduced
in Chapter~\ref{ch:background} into the concrete metrics used to compare
the five synthetic-data generators. The aim is not to define new
evaluation theory, but to fix a reproducible protocol. Every generator is
judged against the same real train/test partitions, the same downstream
models, and the same privacy-audit procedures. The numerical results and
the question of which metrics most clearly separate the generators are
reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\subsection{Two Pillars: Utility and Privacy}
\label{sec:eval:pillars}

Evaluation taxonomies differ in how they position resemblance. Some
medical synthetic-data frameworks treat resemblance, utility, and
privacy as three separate dimensions~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic}, whereas recent surveys group
distributional fidelity and downstream usefulness under a broader
utility or data-availability heading while retaining privacy as a
separate concern~\cite{kaabachi2025scoping,shi2025comprehensive}. This
thesis adopts the latter approach at the top level. Every generator
is assessed along a \emph{utility} pillar and a \emph{privacy} pillar,
with the two reported separately so that trade-offs between them can be
made explicit. To avoid hiding the distinction between resemblance and
task usefulness, the utility pillar is then decomposed into two
complementary branches. The first, \emph{statistical similarity}
(\S\ref{sec:eval:utility:similarity}), asks whether the synthetic table
reproduces the distributional shape of the real data, both the
per-column marginals and the joint structure across columns. The
second, \emph{predictive performance}
(\S\ref{sec:eval:utility:predictive}), asks whether the synthetic table
behaves like the real one when used to train downstream predictive and
survival models. The privacy pillar (\S\ref{sec:eval:privacy}) is
operationalised through instance-level distance metrics and explicit
inference-attack simulations. Section~\ref{sec:eval:aggregate}
introduces the aggregation layer through which the per-metric values
are visualised as a single comparative picture for RQ2 and RQ3.

Two design principles govern the choice of metrics throughout this
chapter. First, the metrics within each branch are deliberately
chosen to be \emph{complementary} in what they measure. Marginal
metric, joint-structure metric, and manifold-geometry metric are
all reported in the similarity branch. A generator may perform 
well on one metric but poorly on another. Therefore,
the metrics must be considered together when answering RQ1, as each captures
a different aspect of synthetic-data quality. Second,
every metric is reported using the same numerical conventions across
generators. train/test split, preprocessing and
standardisation choices, downstream
classifiers, and fixed random seeds for stochastic evaluation
components are kept same. For the differentially private generators, the target
privacy budget is matched at $(\varepsilon,\delta)=(5.0,10^{-5})$.
This uniformity is what makes the cross-generator comparisons in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} sound. The
empirical question of which metrics actually \emph{discriminate}
between the five generators on the Diabetes 130-US Hospitals benchmark,
and which collapse to a single value, is addressed in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq1:diagnostic}. This per-metric, non-composite
reporting aligns with established frameworks such as
SynthEval~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}.

\subsection{Utility Metrics}
\label{sec:eval:utility}

The same synthetic table is evaluated under both statistical-similarity 
and predictive views. This allows a generator with close marginal 
fits but poor predictive performance to be diagnosed clearly. 
Such a generator preserves univariate structure but not conditional structure.

\subsubsection{Statistical Similarity to the Real Data}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity}

\paragraph{Per-Column Distributional Match:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:dist}
The first diagnostic is purely visual. For each column, the empirical 
density of the real and synthetic values is overlaid on the same axis. 
Kernel-density estimates are used for continuous variables. Side-by-side 
bar charts are used for categorical and binary variables. The
purpose of this diagnostic is to expose obvious failure modes that
single-number summaries hide. Following established practice, no
single-number summary is attached to these plots~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023}.
They are reported in
the results chapter (\S\ref{sec:results:rq2:utility:similarity}) and
in an appendix gallery so that any anomaly visible in the
single-number metrics that follow can be cross-checked at the
column level.

\paragraph{Summary Statistics (Mean, Standard Deviation):}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:stats}
The simplest single-number per-column summaries are the sample mean and 
sample standard deviation of each retained column. These summaries are 
computed separately on the real table and on each synthetic table. Moment based and
dimension-wise summary comparisons are common in synthetic health and
tabular-data evaluation~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023,lautrup2025syntheval}.
The pair
$(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{real}}, \hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{real}})$ versus
$(\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{synth}}, \hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{synth}})$ is
informative even though it ignores higher moments and the entire
joint distribution. Large discrepancies in either statistic indicate
that the generator has placed mass on values that do not match the
column's central tendency or spread, which is a much stronger failure
than a per-shape mismatch that preserves the first two moments. Together
these two statistics summarise \emph{low-order marginal moment fidelity}.
They remain blind to all dependencies between columns and to any shape
information beyond the first two moments. In the implementation, the
medication-flag columns and \emph{num\_procedures} are excluded before
this diagnostic is computed. Any remaining column whose real-data mean
and standard deviation are both zero is then removed as degenerate.

\paragraph{Pairwise Correlation Difference (PCD):}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:pcd}

PCD has been used to evaluate correlation preservation in synthetic
patient data~\cite{goncalves2020generation}. The original metric uses
the Frobenius norm to compare the Pearson correlation matrices of the
real and synthetic datasets. This thesis reports an averaged variant,
calculated as the mean absolute difference between the correlations of
all unique column pairs, excluding self-correlations. Lower values
indicate better preservation of the pairwise linear relationships found
in the real data.

However, PCD does not directly assess marginal distributions, does not
reliably capture nonlinear dependencies, and does not represent
higher-order relationships involving more than two variables. Therefore,
a low PCD alone does not confirm that the complete joint distribution
has been preserved. The signed differences for individual column pairs are also presented
as correlation-difference heatmaps. These heatmaps identify which
pairwise correlations were not reproduced accurately and show whether
each correlation was overestimated, underestimated, or reversed in sign.


\paragraph{Kolmogorov-Smirnov Two-Sample Test:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:ks}

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) two-sample statistic compares the
distributions of a real column and its synthetic counterpart. It measures
the largest difference between their cumulative distribution functions.
The statistic ranges from zero to one, where lower values indicate more
similar distributions and zero indicates identical
distributions.

The KS statistic is calculated separately for each column and then
averaged to provide an overall measure of marginal fidelity. Thus, the
mean KS summarises the average of the largest distributional differences
observed within individual columns. It can detect differences in
distributional shape that may not be visible from the mean and standard
deviation alone. However, because each column is evaluated independently,
the statistic does not measure relationships between columns.

Medication-flag columns are excluded because the KS statistic is less
informative for highly concentrated discrete variables. KS is commonly
used as a univariate fidelity measure in synthetic-data
research~\cite{Murtaza2023}.

\paragraph{PCA-Based Manifold Overlap:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:similarity:pca}

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is used to visually compare the
overall structure of the real and synthetic data. This type of visual
comparison is commonly used in the evaluation of synthetic health and
tabular data~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023,lautrup2025syntheval}.

The real and synthetic tables are first combined and their columns are
$z$-score standardised. PCA is then fitted to the combined data, ensuring
that both datasets are projected onto the same coordinate system. The
records are plotted using the first two principal components and
generator-specific colours. For readability, the real data and each
synthetic dataset are subsampled to at most $5{,}000$ records.

The resulting plot provides a visual indication of
\emph{low-dimensional geometric overlap}. It can reveal differences that
may not be detected by the per-column KS statistic or pairwise
correlations. However, overlap in two principal components does not
confirm complete distributional fidelity because information contained
in the remaining dimensions is not shown. Therefore, PCA is treated as
a visual cross-check rather than a primary summary metric.

\subsubsection{Predictive Performance of Downstream Models}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive}

Predictive utility is evaluated through three complementary diagnostics:
classifier-based downstream performance, feature-importance preservation,
and Cox proportional-hazards agreement. The first diagnostic contains the
train-test regimes, classifier suite, scalar metrics, and ROC analysis.
The latter two check whether the learned signal and clinically
interpretable associations are preserved.

\paragraph{Classifier-Based Downstream Performance:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:tstr}
Predictive utility is evaluated primarily with TSTR, 
a model-based utility protocol introduced in prior
work~\cite{Esteban2017TSTR} and catalogued in synthetic-data
reviews and evaluation frameworks~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023,lautrup2025syntheval}.
In TSTR, a classifier is trained on the synthetic table and evaluated
on the held-out real test split. Its performance is compared with the
Train-on-Real, Test-on-Real (TRTR) baseline, where the same classifier
is trained on the real training split and evaluated on the same real
test split. The gap between TSTR and TRTR therefore measures how much
downstream predictive utility is lost through synthesis.

TRTS (Train on Real, Test on Synthetic) and TSTS (Train on Synthetic,
Test on Synthetic) are reported as complementary checks. TRTS asks
whether synthetic test records are realistic enough for a real-trained
classifier, while TSTS checks whether the synthetic train and synthetic
test splits are internally consistent. In this thesis, all five active
generators provide synthetic test splits, so TRTS and TSTS are computed
alongside TSTR for each generator.

The TSTR-TRTR gap estimates the loss in task-specific predictive
utility associated with training on synthetic rather than real data
under the selected readmission outcome, classifiers, preprocessing
procedure, and test split. It does not measure general distributional
fidelity or clinical validity.

\paragraph{Classifier Suite:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:classifiers}
Three downstream classifiers are evaluated, representing linear,
tree ensemble, and gradient-boosting methods.
Logistic regression with default $\ell_2$ regularisation and up to
$1{,}000$ optimisation iterations, a random forest with $100$ trees
under default split settings, and gradient boosting with $100$ boosting
rounds. Reporting all three rather than a single ``best'' classifier
limits the risk that a generator's utility ranking is an artefact of
the particular inductive bias of one classifier, a concern raised in
synthetic-data research~\cite{Murtaza2023}. At the classifier stage, 
any remaining missing values are handled using
median imputation fitted on the current training source and applied to the
corresponding test table. The source tables had, however, already undergone
imputation during preprocessing before the train-test split. 
This guards against information leakage
from test statistics into the imputation. All classifiers are seeded
with a fixed random state so that the cross-generator comparison is
not perturbed by run-to-run variance within a classifier.

\paragraph{Performance Metrics:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:metrics}
For each combination of training source and classifier, five metrics are
reported: accuracy, precision, recall, AUC--ROC, and the binary F1 score.
The readmission indicator is treated as the positive class.
The full five-metric set is retained because accuracy alone is
misleading on the moderately imbalanced readmission target. F1 and
the precision/recall pair expose threshold-dependent failure modes
that AUC-ROC averages out, and AUC-ROC in turn reflects
threshold-independent ranking quality that F1 ignores. In short,
accuracy summarises \emph{aggregate correctness of the thresholded
decision rule}, F1 the \emph{balance between false positives and false negatives at
the chosen threshold}, and AUC-ROC the \emph{threshold-independent
ranking quality}. A generator can score well on one of these and
poorly on the others, so they are treated as complementary rather
than as alternative scalarisations of the same underlying quantity.

\paragraph{ROC Curve Analysis:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:roc}
The gradient-boosting classifier is additionally used to generate
ROC-curve overlays for the TRTR baseline and each TSTR generator.
The overlay diagnoses whether a TSTR generator's gap to the baseline 
is concentrated at a particular operating point along the curve. 
It also shows whether the gap is spread uniformly across thresholds. 
This information is collapsed away by the scalar AUC-ROC reported in 
the previous paragraph.

\paragraph{Feature-Importance Preservation:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:fi}
A complementary model-based diagnostic compares the random-forest
feature-importance profiles obtained from real and synthetic training
data. Random forests are used because they provide an importance value
for every feature, while gradient boosting AUC remains the primary
measure of predictive performance.

For each training source, the feature importances are normalised to sum
to one. The profiles are compared visually using side-by-side bars and
summarised by a feature-importance agreement score between zero and one.
A score of one indicates identical allocations of importance, whereas
zero indicates that the two models assign importance to entirely
different feature sets.

The score measures agreement in the allocation of feature-importance
mass, rather than exact feature rankings or clinical importance. The
mean-decrease-in-impurity importances used here may favour variables
with more possible split points and may vary between fitted forests.
Therefore, the score is treated as an interpretability-oriented utility
diagnostic rather than a stand-alone measure of feature relevance.
Similar feature-importance comparisons have been used in PATE-GAN and
clinical synthetic-data evaluations~\cite{pategan,beaulieu2019privacy}.

\paragraph{Cox-Based Real-Synthetic Comparison:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox}
A Cox proportional-hazards model is fitted separately to the real
training table and each synthetic training table~\cite{Cox1972PH}.
Within this model, the recorded length of stay (\emph{time in hospital})
is treated as the duration, and the binary readmission indicator is
treated as the event. Thirteen predefined covariates are included. A
penaliser of $0.01$ is applied to improve numerical stability. 
The resulting per-covariate hazard ratios and their
$95\%$ confidence intervals are then compared between the real and
synthetic data.

TThe analysis assesses whether the synthetic tables reproduce the
pattern of coefficients obtained from the real table under the same
Cox-model specification. Comparisons of Cox-model
estimates obtained from real and synthetic health data have similarly
been used as utility diagnostics in previous evaluations
\cite{healthgan,yale2020synthesizing}.

\paragraph{Cox Confidence-Interval Overlap:}
\label{sec:eval:utility:predictive:cox-overlap}
The available Cox hazard ratios for each generator are summarised
against the real-data Cox ratios by computing. For each shared covariate,
the overlap between the real-fit $95\%$ confidence interval and the
synthetic-fit $95\%$ confidence interval. The reported summary is
the fraction of covariates for which the two intervals overlap. 
A generator that preserves survival-related associations should produce
hazard-ratio confidence intervals that overlap those estimated from the
real data. Non-overlapping intervals may indicate that these associations
have been distorted for the affected covariates. This
overlap fraction expresses \emph{agreement at the level of clinically
interpretable effect sizes} and is therefore a clinically interpretable
summary in the predictive branch.

\subsection{Privacy Metrics}
\label{sec:eval:privacy}

Privacy is evaluated using two complementary groups of metrics.
Instance-level distance metrics measure how closely synthetic records
resemble real records in the feature space. Inference attacks simulate
specific adversarial attempts to extract information from the synthetic
data. Both are included because they examine different privacy risks.
For example, records may be far from individual real records but still
reveal membership information through broader distance patterns.
Conversely, a generator may resist a particular inference attack while
leaving unusual records vulnerable to nearest-neighbour linkage.

These metrics provide empirical evidence against specific attacks and
proximity-based risks, but they do not establish a formal privacy
guarantee. For the differentially private generators, the
$(\varepsilon,\delta)$ budget described in \S\ref{sec:bg:dp} and
summarised in \S\ref{sec:methods:summary} provides the formal guarantee,
while the empirical metrics offer complementary evidence. For the
non-private generators, the metrics provide empirical privacy evidence
without a formal guarantee. Before the distance-based metrics are
calculated, numeric columns are $z$-score standardised so that variables
with larger numerical ranges do not dominate the distance calculations.

\paragraph{Nearest-Neighbour Distance:}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:nn}

Nearest-neighbour distance measures the similarity between real and
synthetic records in the standardised feature space. 
Two directions are considered: the distance from each synthetic
record to its nearest real record
(\emph{synthetic-to-real distance}, $d_{S\to R}$) and the distance from
each real record to its nearest synthetic record
(\emph{real-to-synthetic distance}, $d_{R\to S}$).

The median and fifth percentile of both distance distributions are
reported. The median represents the proximity of a typical record.
The fifth percentile focuses on the records with the smallest
distances. This lower tail is particularly relevant to privacy because
unusually close synthetic and real records may indicate a greater risk
of record linkage or re-identification. However, proximity alone does
not demonstrate that re-identification is possible.

Nearest-neighbour distance therefore measures
\emph{record-level proximity}, which is relevant to distance based
linkage attacks discussed in the synthetic-data privacy
literature~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,goncalves2020generation,wang2019generating}.
Real-to-real nearest-neighbour distances are also calculated as a
reference. 

\paragraph{Nearest-Neighbour Distance Ratio (NNDR):}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:nndr}
NNDR normalises the $d_{S\to R}$
described above by the natural spacing between real records. For each
synthetic record, its distance to the nearest real record is divided by
a randomly selected real-to-real nearest-neighbour distance. A small
constant is added to the denominator to prevent division by zero, and
the median ratio is reported for each generator.

This implementation differs from the SynthEval
variant~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}. SynthEval compares the distances
from a synthetic record to its two nearest real records. In contrast, 
this thesis compares each synthetic-to-real
nearest-neighbour distance with a real-to-real
nearest-neighbour distance. Values close to one indicate
similar spacing, while values below one may suggest unusually close
records and possible memorisation. Values above one indicate greater
separation, but may also result from poor data fidelity. NNDR is
therefore interpreted together with the utility results.

\paragraph{Nearest-Neighbour Adversarial Accuracy (NNAA):}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:nnaa}

Nearest-Neighbour Adversarial Accuracy (NNAA) measures how easily real
and synthetic records can be distinguished using their
nearest-neighbour relationships~\cite{healthgan}. For each record, the
method compares its distance to the closest record in the same dataset
with its distance to the closest record in the other dataset. The final
score summarises these comparisons across the real and synthetic
records.

A value close to $0.5$ indicates that the two datasets are difficult to
distinguish using this nearest-neighbour rule. A value below $0.5$ may
indicate unusually close real-synthetic pairs and possible
memorisation. A value above $0.5$ indicates greater separation between
the datasets and may reflect distributional mismatch.

The analysis also reports \emph{PrivacyLoss}, calculated as the
difference between the test and training NNAA scores, following prior
work~\cite{healthgan}. A positive value indicates that the synthetic
data resemble the training subset more closely than the comparison
subset and may therefore suggest memorisation. A value close to zero
could mean an ideal privacy achievement but this has to be interpreted
with caution and have to be evaluated with utility metrics. 
When both training and test NNAA values are close to one but
\emph{PrivacyLoss} is near zero, the synthetic data are similarly
distant from both real subsets, indicating distributional mismatch
rather than strong privacy. Both NNAA and
PrivacyLoss are treated as empirical diagnostics rather than formal
privacy guarantees.

\paragraph{Membership Inference Attack:}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:mia}

A membership inference attack examines whether a candidate record was
included in the data used to train the generator
\cite{Shokri2017MIA}. For each candidate, the distances to its first,
third, and fifth nearest synthetic neighbours, together with their mean,
are used as attack features. Up to 5,000 training records are labelled
as members and an equal number of test records as non-members.

A logistic-regression attacker is evaluated using five-fold
cross-validation, and its performance is measured using
$\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{MIA}}$. The signed attacker advantage is
calculated as
$\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{MIA}}
= \mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{MIA}}-0.5$.
A value of zero represents random performance, a positive value
indicates increasing ability to identify members, and a negative value
indicates an AUC below the random benchmark. The thresholds $0.05$ and
$0.10$ are used as descriptive boundaries for low, medium, and high
risk. This metric therefore provides an empirical assessment of
membership leakage under the specified attack
\cite{Murtaza2023,hernandez2022synthetic}.

\paragraph{Attribute Inference Attack:}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:aia}

An attribute inference attack tests whether a sensitive attribute can
be predicted from the remaining attributes and the synthetic data
\cite{Murtaza2023}. Four attributes are examined: readmission status,
gender, age, and primary diagnosis category. For each attribute, up to
5,000 real records are matched to their ten nearest synthetic
neighbours using the other columns. The sensitive value is then
predicted by majority vote among those neighbours.

The attack accuracy is compared with a baseline that always predicts
the most common value. The adversary advantage is calculated as
$\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{AIA}}
= \mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{AIA}}
- \mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{majority}}$.
A positive value indicates that the attack outperforms the baseline,
zero indicates equal performance, and a negative value indicates worse
performance. This metric provides an empirical assessment of
attribute-disclosure risk and complements the predictive-utility
evaluation.

\paragraph{Clustering Proximity and Outlier Linkability:}
\label{sec:eval:privacy:linkability}

Outlier records may face re-identification risk because their
unusual characteristics can make them easier to distinguish
\cite{stadler2022synthetic,wang2019generating}. In the standardised
feature space, real records are ranked by their Euclidean distance from
the real-data centroid. The most distant five per cent are treated as
outliers, and an equal-sized random sample of non-outliers is used for
comparison.

The proximity radius is set to the seventy-fifth percentile of the
real-to-real nearest-neighbour distances. For each generator, the
analysis calculates the proportion of outliers and non-outliers with at
least one synthetic neighbour within this radius. The reported gap is
the non-outlier linkability minus the outlier linkability. A negative
gap indicates greater proximity around outliers, while a positive gap
indicates greater proximity around non-outliers. This metric therefore
provides an empirical assessment of whether synthetic records are
disproportionately concentrated around unusual real records.

\subsection{Comparative Aggregation}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate}

The utility and privacy metrics defined in \S\ref{sec:eval:utility} and
\S\ref{sec:eval:privacy} are brought together through four comparative
views: a summary table, a privacy-utility landscape, a utility radar,
and a utility-privacy heatmap. These views make the differences between
the generators easier to inspect, but they do not replace the original
metric values. The conclusions therefore remain based on the individual
metrics rather than on a single overall score. This use of complementary
metrics is consistent with established synthetic-data evaluation
frameworks~\cite{hernandez2022synthetic,Murtaza2023,lautrup2025syntheval},
while the multivariate risk-utility displays follow prior
work~\cite{thees2025beyond}.

\paragraph{Comprehensive Summary Table:}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:table}
The summary table reports twelve main metrics for the five generators
and the real-data TRTR reference where applicable.
The rows are grouped into statistical similarity, predictive utility,
and privacy. Values are shown on their original scales together with
their relevant direction or reference value. The table does not combine
the metrics into an overall score or rank. It also supports the RQ1
analysis (\S\ref{sec:results:rq1:empirical}), which examines how much
each metric varies across the evaluated generators.

\paragraph{Privacy-Utility Landscape:}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:tradeoff}
The landscape plots gradient-boosting TSTR AUC-ROC on the utility axis
and median NNDR on the distance-based privacy axis. Each generator is
shown as one point, with the real-data AUC-ROC and NNDR unity value used
as reference lines. The same plane is used to identify non-dominated
points for RQ2 (\S\ref{sec:results:rq2:landscape}) and to show the
observed changes between the selected private and non-private models for
RQ3 (\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:arrow-plot}). These arrows describe the
differences between the fitted models; they do not isolate differential
privacy as the only possible cause of those differences.

This landscape is a two-metric summary rather than a complete measure of
utility or privacy. In particular, an NNDR far above one may indicate
that the synthetic data have moved away from the real-data distribution,
not that they provide stronger usable privacy. The attack-based privacy
results and the remaining utility metrics must therefore be considered
alongside the plot. Similarly, membership of the selected Pareto frontier
does not establish that a generator is secure or generally superior. The
risk-utility map is used as a descriptive comparison following prior
work~\cite{thees2025beyond}.


\paragraph{Utility Radar:}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:radar}
The radar chart compares six utility measures: AUC-ROC, F1,
$1-$mean KS, $1-$correlation difference, Cox confidence-interval
overlap, and feature-importance agreement. Each measure is min-max
scaled to $[0,1]$, with the real-data TRTR result used as a scaling
anchor where applicable. Larger values indicate stronger relative
utility on the corresponding axis.

The scaled values depend on the models included in the comparison. A
value of zero therefore marks the lowest observed value, not necessarily
an absence of utility, while a value of one marks the highest observed
value rather than perfect performance. Privacy metrics are excluded
because several have a reference point or a two-sided interpretation and
cannot be represented reliably as ``larger is better''. The radar is
therefore interpreted as a qualitative utility profile, not as a score or
ranking based on polygon size. This type of directionally aligned radial
display follows prior work~\cite{thees2025beyond}.

\paragraph{Utility-Privacy Heatmap:}
\label{sec:eval:aggregate:heatmap}
The heatmap places the utility and privacy results on a common visual
scale. Each metric is min-max scaled to $[0,1]$ across the five
generators. For utility, larger values indicate stronger relative
performance. For privacy, the values are oriented as disclosure risk, so
larger values indicate greater concern under the corresponding
diagnostic.

NNDR contributes the following memorisation-risk value:
\[
  \max(0,\,1-\mathrm{NNDR}).
\]
For NNAA test accuracy, only values below the $0.5$ reference contribute
to the risk term:
\[
  \max(0,\,1-2\,\mathrm{AA}_{\mathrm{test}}).
\]
For membership-inference AUC, only values above $0.5$ contribute:
\[
  \max(0,\,2\,\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{MIA}}-1).
\]
Larger maximum attribute-inference advantage and outlier linkability are
treated as greater risk, while only the positive part of NNAA privacy
loss is included.

These transformations are specific to this analysis. They count
near-record resemblance as privacy risk, but they do not reward large
distances as evidence of stronger privacy. A generator that produces data
far from the real distribution may therefore appear low-risk in this
block even when its utility is poor. The heatmap must consequently be
read together with the raw summary table, the per-metric privacy results,
and the landscape. Its scaled values show relative differences within
this set of generators. They are not absolute risk probabilities or a
formal privacy guarantee. The heatmap is used for metric-level comparison,
not as an overall ranking~\cite{thees2025beyond}.
