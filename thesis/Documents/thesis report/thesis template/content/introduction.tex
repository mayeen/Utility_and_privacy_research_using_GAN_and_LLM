\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro:motivation}

% Migrated and lightly edited from proposal §1 (Introduction) + §2 (Motivation).
The growth of digital health records, in together with rapid advances
in artificial intelligence (AI), has significant potential to transform
healthcare. At the same time, regulatory frameworks such as the
GDPR and HIPAA play a crucial role in safeguarding patient's 
confidential data and ensuring responsible data use. These protections are
important for maintaining public trust in digital health systems. They also
drive researchers to explore innovative methods that enable data-driven
innovation while upholding proper privacy standards. Conventional anonymisation
methods have repeatedly proven insufficient to guarantee privacy in
high-dimensional health data because of re-identification and linkage 
attacks~\cite{Murtaza2023,stadler2022synthetic}.

Synthetic data generation (SDG) has emerged as a promising alternative.
It enables the creation of artificial datasets that retain the statistical
properties of the original while mitigating privacy risks. Among different methods, the most
prominent are \emph{Generative Adversarial Networks} (GANs) and
\emph{Large Language Models} (LLMs). GANs have demonstrated success in
structured medical records~\cite{Choi2017MedGAN,Xu2019CTGAN}, while transformer-based LLMs
offer advantages in modelling heterogeneous
records~\cite{Miletic2024}. GANs work by training
two neural networks. It has a generator that creates synthetic samples and a
discriminator that tries to distinguish them from real data until the
generator learns to produce highly realistic outputs. LLMs
operate using transformer architectures that leverage self-attention to learn
long-range patterns in sequences. This method allows them to generate coherent text or
structured records. While GANs focus on matching data distributions through
adversarial learning, LLMs generate data by predicting the next most probable
token based on context.

The need for privacy-preserving data generation in healthcare is not only a
technical challenge but also a foundational concern of medical research. The
scarcity of openly accessible, high-quality clinical data hinders
innovation in diagnosis, treatment planning, and epidemiological modelling.
Responsible data sharing in healthcare requires strong safeguards for patient
privacy and ethical use of sensitive records. Synthetic data presents a path to mitigate
this bottleneck by allowing the distribution of data without direct disclosure
of personal identifiers. This enables accelerating AI model development and
validation.

\section{Problem Statement: The Utility-Privacy Tension}
\label{sec:intro:problem}

The adoption of synthetic data in healthcare depends on two interdependent
requirements: utility and privacy~\cite{kaabachi2025scoping}. Synthetic data is
valuable only if it remains analytically useful, and defensible only if it
reduces the risk of exposing information about real patients. The core problem
is therefore not to optimise either criterion in isolation, but to understand
the trade-off between them, which prior work has shown to be difficult to
predict~\cite{stadler2022synthetic}.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0.25em
    \item \textbf{Utility:} the dataset must preserve the statistical,
        structural, and predictive characteristics needed for downstream
        analysis~\cite{kaabachi2025scoping}, including marginal feature
        distributions, variable dependencies, target class balance, and model
        generalisation to real held-out data.
  \item \textbf{Privacy:} the dataset must provide resistance to adversarial
        attacks, such as membership inference, attribute disclosure, and
        record-level linkage~\cite{kaabachi2025scoping}, especially when rare
        diagnoses, unusual treatment patterns, or repeated encounters make
        patients identifiable even after direct identifiers have been removed.
\end{enumerate}
While GANs and LLMs each offer unique strengths, there is limited comparative
work evaluating their effectiveness under a unified privacy-utility framework,
particularly for heterogeneous healthcare datasets.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro:rqs}

This thesis addresses three research questions.

\paragraph{RQ1:}
\label{rq:rq1} Which methods and metrics are suitable for evaluating the
utility (statistical resemblance and predictive performance) and privacy risk
of synthetic tabular medical data, and what distinct dimension of data
characteristics does each capture?

No single metric can quantify the quality of a synthetic dataset. A
generator may reproduce marginal feature distributions faithfully yet distort
the dependencies between variables. Similarly, it may support accurate
downstream prediction while memorising individual patient records. RQ1
asks which established measures are needed to expose such failures.
In this thesis, a set of metrics is considered \emph{suitable} if it meets
two criteria. Firstly, it must cover the failure modes documented in the
literature: distributional distortion, loss of variable dependencies,
degraded predictive performance, memorisation, and vulnerability to
membership-inference, attribute-inference, and linkage attacks. Secondly, each
metric must capture a distinct dimension of data quality, such that no metric
is redundant with the others. The second criterion is also examined
empirically. The metrics that rank the generators differently cannot be measuring
the same property. The answer to RQ1 takes the form of the unified two-pillar
evaluation framework defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, which provides
the instruments used to answer the remaining two questions.

\paragraph{RQ2:}
\label{rq:rq2} How do GAN-based and LLM-based synthesisers compare on the
privacy-utility trade-off when applied to tabular medical data, and which
achieves a more effective balance?

GANs and LLMs generate data through fundamentally different mechanisms.
GANs match the data distribution through adversarial training, whereas LLMs
predict records token by token. There is thus no reason to expect the two
families to share the same strengths or the same failure modes. RQ2 applies
the framework from RQ1 to compare both families directly on the
\emph{Diabetes 130-US Hospitals} dataset. Here, a more effective balance
means retaining analytical utility without a correspondingly higher privacy
risk. A generator that excels on one criterion at the expense of the other
does not satisfy this standard. The comparison is reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\paragraph{RQ3:}
\label{rq:rq3} At a matched privacy budget ($\varepsilon = 5.0$), how does enforcing
differential privacy using family-appropriate mechanisms affect utility, and
how do the resulting privacy-utility profiles differ between GAN-based
synthesisers (PATE-GAN compared with HealthGAN) and LLM-based synthesisers
(Pythia-1B-DP compared with Pythia-1B) for tabular medical data?

RQ2 compares the two generator families in general, but RQ3 is isolated to the
effect of a formal differential-privacy guarantee. The two model families support 
different mechanisms for enforcing differential
privacy. In this thesis, PATE is used in the GAN-based settingand DP-SGD is used in the
LLM-based setting. The comparison therefore fixes the privacy budget at
$\varepsilon = 5.0$, so that both families operate under an equivalent formal
guarantee. Each differentially private model is then compared with its
non-DP counterpart. This means comparing 
PATE-GAN against HealthGAN for GAN-based synthesisers. For LLM-based synthesisers, 
we are comparing Pythia-1B-DP against Pythia-1B. 
This within-family contrast quantifies the utility cost of
differential privacy. The cross-family contrast shows which mechanism incurs
the smaller utility loss at the same budget.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro:contributions}

This thesis makes four main contributions to the evaluation of
privacy-preserving synthetic tabular health data.

First, it develops a reproducible evaluation pipeline for synthetic medical
tabular data. The pipeline is organised around two complementary dimensions:
\emph{utility} and \emph{privacy}. The \emph{utility} dimension comprises
both statistical similarity to the real data and the
predictive performance of downstream models. The \emph{privacy} dimension covers
proximity-based memorisation indicators, membership-inference risk,
attribute-inference risk, and outlier linkability.

Second, it provides a head-to-head empirical comparison of five generators on
the \emph{Diabetes 130-US Hospitals} dataset: HealthGAN
(WGAN-GP), PATE-GAN
($(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP), Pythia-70M-LoRA and
Pythia-1B-LoRA, and
Pythia-1B-DP-LoRA using DP-SGD via
Opacus.

Third, it quantifies the marginal cost of formal differential privacy within
both model families. Within the GAN family, the comparison is between non-private HealthGAN
and PATE-GAN at $\varepsilon=5.0$. Within the LLM family, the comparison
is between non-private Pythia-1B and Pythia-1B-DP at same privacy budget.
The inclusion of the non-DP Pythia-1B
scale anchor and the Pythia-1B-DP variant constitutes a scope extension beyond
the originally submitted proposal, motivated by the finding that DP-SGD applied
to Pythia-70M does not converge usefully at this privacy budget
(\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:pythia-70m-failure}).

Fourth, based on the evaluated dataset, model configurations, privacy budgets, 
and selected utility and privacy metrics, it derives practical recommendations 
for medical data stewards. These
recommendations identify when a non-DP GAN may be sufficient, which
differentially private mechanism is preferable when a formal guarantee is
required, and under which privacy-budget and governance constraints LLM-based
synthesis is competitive.

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:intro:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows.
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:background}} surveys the background and related work
on tabular synthetic data generation, GAN- and LLM-based approaches,
differential privacy, and existing evaluation frameworks.
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}} consolidates the experimental
methodology of the thesis. Section~\ref{sec:method:data} describes the
\emph{Diabetes 130-US Hospitals} benchmark and the feature-engineering
pipeline used to prepare it for synthesis.
Section~\ref{sec:method:generators} details the five generators compared in
this work: HealthGAN, PATE-GAN, Pythia-70M-LoRA, Pythia-1B-LoRA, and
Pythia-1B-DP-LoRA, including architectures, training protocols, and the
differentially private mechanisms employed. Section~\ref{sec:method:evaluation} defines the unified
two-pillar evaluation framework: utility (statistical similarity and
predictive performance) and privacy.
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:results}} reports the empirical findings, structured
around the three research questions.
\textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}} interprets the results, discusses
strengths and weaknesses of each generator, and acknowledges threats to
validity. Finally, \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}} summarises the
contributions, returns directly to RQ1-RQ3, and outlines possible extensions
for future work.
