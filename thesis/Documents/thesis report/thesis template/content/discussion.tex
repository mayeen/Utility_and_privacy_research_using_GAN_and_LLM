\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

% Where the Results chapter reports numbers, this chapter interprets them,
% situates the findings against prior work, and acknowledges limits.

\section{Interpretation of Results per Research Question}
\label{sec:discussion:interpretation}

The findings for RQ1 confirm that the twelve-metric, two-pillar framework
is not redundant: each metric isolates a dimension of data quality that the
others miss, and the clearest empirical demonstration is the behaviour of
PATE-GAN. It attained apparently ideal scores on every distance-based
privacy metric (a median NNDR of $105$ and zero outlier linkability) while
failing every utility metric, a combination that is legible only because
the framework reports utility and privacy as separate, jointly necessary
pillars. A single composite score, or privacy metrics read in isolation,
would have mislabelled a generator that produces unusable data as the most
private and therefore the best. This is the concrete form of the warning by
\textcite{hernandez2022synthetic} that no single method or metric is
universally adequate, and it justifies the deliberate breadth of the suite.

For RQ2, the central observation is that neither generator family dominates
the privacy-utility plane, and the mechanistic reason lies in what each
paradigm optimises. The WGAN-GP objective of HealthGAN matches the real
distribution directly in a continuous latent space, which yields the lowest
marginal discrepancy (mean KS $0.0480$) and the only usable operating point
that sits at or above the NNDR unity reference on the median scale. The
token-based Pythia models
instead learn the conditional structure of the table autoregressively,
which yields the strongest predictive transferability (Pythia-1B TSTR AUC
$0.682$, nearest the real ceiling) and the best joint-dependency fidelity.
The recurring F1 inversion, in which synthetic-trained classifiers exceed
the real-trained baseline's F1, reflects not better discrimination
but an operating-point shift toward the minority class, and it illustrates
why threshold-dependent and threshold-independent predictive metrics are
both reported. \textcite{Miletic2024} document continued utility gains
with language-model scale, which suggests that the LLM advantage on the
predictive axis could widen at scales larger than those evaluated here. That high utility
need not coincide with strong privacy is consistent with the independent
benchmark of SynthEval, in which the highest-utility generators ranked among the
worst on privacy~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}.

For RQ3, the asymmetry in how the two families absorb differential privacy
follows from where the privacy mechanism acts. On the LLM side, DP-SGD
perturbs the gradients of a small low-rank adapter, concentrating a fixed
privacy budget over relatively few trainable parameters. The resulting cost
is contained (a TSTR AUC drop of $0.099$) and leaves distributional fidelity
and survival-model agreement essentially intact. That this works at the
$1$B scale but not at $70$M is exactly the pattern predicted by
\textcite{Li2022LargeDP}, who show that larger language models tolerate the
noise injected by DP-SGD more gracefully. The convergence failure at the
$70$M scale (\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:pythia-70m-failure}) is the negative
confirmation. Although the GAN-side collapse under differential privacy, but it
cannot be read as a pure DP effect, because HealthGAN and PATE-GAN differ in
architecture as well as in privacy mechanism
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:limit:gan-toggle-asymmetry}). The results suggest that,
 at $\varepsilon = 5.0$, the evaluated DP-GAN baseline did not retain sufficient 
 utility for this task, whereas the DP-LLM retained usable performance.

\section{Strengths and Weaknesses of the Two Generator Families}
\label{sec:discussion:gan-vs-llm}

Viewed as paradigms rather than individual models, the two families exhibit
complementary strengths that the results make concrete. The strength of the
GAN family is distributional: HealthGAN matches the real marginals more
tightly than any other generator and, once trained, samples cheaply. Its
weaknesses are equally visible here: it enforces no schema-level
constraints, and the only differentially private GAN evaluated does not
produce usable data at the tested budget. Although that outcome conflates
the differential-privacy mechanism with an architecture change and cannot
be attributed to differential privacy alone
(\S\ref{sec:discussion:limit:gan-toggle-asymmetry}). The strength of the LLM family
is its native handling of mixed-type tabular data through a row-as-text
representation: the same model ingests categorical, ordinal, and continuous
fields without per-column encoders, learns their conditional structure, and
through schema-aware parsing, emits syntactically valid rows. This
representational flexibility underlies the predictive advantage of the LLM
family. Its graceful degradation under differential privacy has a distinct
cause, namely that DP-SGD perturbs a small low-rank adapter at the $1$B
scale, rather than following from the text representation itself. Its
costs are a generation-time parsing-and-retry overhead, substantially longer
training and inference, and a known susceptibility to word-for-word memorisation
of training text~\cite{Carlini2021Extracting}. Notably, however, the
membership-inference results reported here show no measurable memorisation by
any generator at this scale (\S\ref{sec:results:rq2:privacy:mia}).

\paragraph{Sensitivity to Categorical Granularity:}
The age-binning ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:results:rq2:utility:age-binning}) shows that upstream
categorical-cardinality choices can affect generators unequally, although
the comparison is limited to the two GAN baselines and Pythia-70M, the only
models for which matched three-bin and ten-bin runs exist. Restoring the
native ten-decade \emph{age} encoding raised the Gradient-Boosting TSTR
AUC of Pythia-70M by $\approx 0.065$ and its F1 by $\approx 0.106$, the
largest AUC gain among the compared generators. HealthGAN responded on a
similar scale but in a mixed direction, gaining on F1 ($+0.118$) while
losing slightly on AUC ($-0.029$), whereas PATE-GAN remained weak under
both encodings. No single family is therefore uniformly more sensitive on
this ablation. The practical implication for healthcare data stewards is
that preprocessing decisions which flatten ordinal categorical variables
can shift recoverable downstream utility for some generators, and should be
validated against the native encoding of the dataset before being committed
to.

\section{Practical Recommendations for Healthcare Data Stewards}
\label{sec:discussion:recommendations}

The results translate into a small set of evidence-based recommendations
for a data steward choosing a generator for tabular medical data.

\paragraph{When a formal privacy guarantee is required:}
The differentially private LLM (Pythia-1B-DP) is the recommended choice
rather than the differentially private GAN. At $\varepsilon = 5.0$ the
DP-LLM retained usable utility (TSTR AUC $0.583$) and reduced attribute
disclosure, whereas PATE-GAN collapsed to near chance on every utility
metric. This inverts the conventional default of reaching for the de-facto
DP-GAN baseline, subject to the caveat that the DP-LLM must be operated at
sufficient scale, since DP-SGD failed outright at the $70$M scale.

\paragraph{When maximum utility is required without a formal guarantee:}
Two options are defensible. Pythia-1B gives the highest predictive utility
and the best joint-dependency fidelity, while HealthGAN gives the best
marginal fidelity and the only non-private operating point at or above the
NNDR unity reference, at a fraction of the computational cost of the LLM.
The choice is the genuine trade-off identified in RQ2 rather than a clear
winner.

\paragraph{When schema handling or operational simplicity dominates:}
The LLM generators ingest mixed-type data without per-column encoders and
emit schema-valid rows directly, which simplifies the pipeline at the cost
of generation time; the GAN requires an explicit encoding and decoding stage
but is far cheaper to run.

\paragraph{Outlier protection:}
No generator concentrated synthetic mass around the vulnerable tail of the
real distribution (outlier linkability $\leq 0.6\%$ for all), so outlier
disclosure was not a discriminating factor in this study. A steward with a
specific outlier-protection requirement should nonetheless verify it
directly, since this property is dataset-dependent.

\section{Toward Standardisation of Synthetic-Data Evaluation}
\label{sec:discussion:standardisation}

The evaluation framework assembled for this thesis, comprising two utility
branches (statistical similarity and predictive performance) and a privacy
pillar reported through twelve complementary single-number metrics, is
offered as a step toward the reusable evaluation standard called for by
\textcite{hernandez2022synthetic}. General-purpose frameworks such as SynthEval
pursue this goal for arbitrary tabular data~\cite{lautrup2025syntheval}; the
present framework instead specialises the same two-pillar structure to a
clinical benchmark and to a controlled comparison across the GAN and LLM
paradigms. Its central design principle, that the
metrics are jointly necessary rather than individually sufficient,
generalises directly: the PATE-GAN case shows that any framework reporting
fewer dimensions risks rewarding degenerate generators. What would not
transfer unchanged is the domain-specific content of the suite. The Cox
proportional-hazards agreement metric assumes a survival-analytic outcome
and would be replaced by the appropriate downstream task elsewhere; the
privacy thresholds (an NNDR of unity, the outlier radius) are calibrated to
the geometry of the real data and must be recomputed per dataset. The
predictive protocol presumes a defined target variable. The framework should
be read as a reusable \emph{structure}, namely two utility
branches plus privacy interpreted jointly, instantiated here with metrics
appropriate to a binary-outcome clinical table.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

\subsection{Single-Dataset Scope}
\label{sec:discussion:limit:dataset}

The entire study is conducted on a single benchmark, the Diabetes 130-US
Hospitals dataset. It is a deliberate choice of a large
($> 70{,}000$-record), mixed-type clinical table with binarized
outcome, which makes it a representative medical tabular testbed, but it is
not universal: the secondary benchmark of the proposal (MIMIC-III) was
scope-reduced, and the findings, particularly the absolute utility figures
and the family rankings, may not transfer to datasets with very different
dimensionality, sparsity, or outcome structure.

\subsection{Upper Bound on Language-Model Scale}
\label{sec:discussion:limit:pythia-size}

The LLM-side evaluation is capped at the Pythia-1B scale. Although Pythia-1B
was added precisely to provide a larger non-private and DP anchor than
Pythia-70M (and the DP-SGD failure at 70M, reported in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:pythia-70m-failure}, shows why this scale shift was
necessary), larger decoder-only models in the multi-billion-parameter range,
such as Llama-class or Qwen-class models, are not evaluated. The findings on
the LLM side should therefore be read as characterising the small-to-mid LLM
regime; \textcite{Miletic2024} report continued gains with model size, so the
absolute utility figures here are likely a lower bound on what comparable
LLM-based synthesisers can attain at greater scale.

\subsection{Architectural Asymmetry of the Cross-Family DP Comparison}
\label{sec:discussion:limit:gan-toggle-asymmetry}

The GAN-family DP comparison reported in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:gan-toggle} conflates architectural and mechanism
differences: HealthGAN and PATE-GAN are not the same architecture with
differential privacy toggled on or off, but distinct generative designs,
only one of which incorporates it. The LLM-family comparison
(\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:llm-toggle}) is, on the contrary, an
architecture-controlled toggle. Pythia-1B and Pythia-1B-DP share the same
decoder-only backbone, the same Tabula serialisation, and the same LoRA
fine-tuning configuration. So the only material difference between them is
the DP-SGD training procedure. This asymmetry is unavoidable, given both the
absence of a standardised non-private analogue of PATE-GAN and the technical
obstacles to retrofitting DP-SGD onto WGAN-GP discussed in
\S\ref{sec:methods:healthgan:nodp}. It should nonetheless be kept in mind
when interpreting cross-family differences in the cost of DP enforcement,
because part of the GAN-side utility delta in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:gan-toggle} is attributable to architecture rather
than to differential privacy alone.

\subsection{Dual Role of PATE-GAN in the Landscape Comparison}
\label{sec:discussion:limit:pategan-dual-role}

PATE-GAN appears alongside non-private models in the RQ2 landscape view
(\S\ref{sec:results:rq2:landscape}) even though it incorporates differential
privacy by construction. This is a reporting choice rather than a flaw:
PATE-GAN is the de-facto DP-GAN baseline in the literature~\cite{pategan},
and excluding it from the landscape figure would obscure where the GAN
family lands once formal DP is required. Its position on the privacy-utility
plane should be read accordingly: it is the DP-enforced operating point of
the GAN family, whereas the non-private generators (HealthGAN, Pythia-70M,
and Pythia-1B) sit on the plane without any formal guarantee.

\subsection{Shared Experimental Outputs Across RQ2 and RQ3}
\label{sec:discussion:limit:shared-data}

RQ2 and RQ3 do not correspond to separate experiments. RQ3 is a
within-family re-analysis of the same five generator outputs reported in
RQ2 (the four-generator subset HealthGAN, PATE-GAN, Pythia-1B, and
Pythia-1B-DP), viewed through a DP-toggle lens rather than a landscape lens.
The shared experimental base means the two research questions are not
statistically independent estimates of generator behaviour. This is by
design, but it constrains the conclusions in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:answer} to within-run statements rather than
cross-run reproducibility claims.

\subsection{Single Privacy Budget ($\varepsilon = 5.0$)}
\label{sec:discussion:limit:epsilon}

Both differentially private generators are evaluated at a single privacy
budget, $\varepsilon = 5.0$ with $\delta = 10^{-5}$. This fixes the
comparison at one point on the privacy-utility curve and cannot reveal its
slope. A budget sweep (for example $\varepsilon \in \{1, 3, 5, 10\}$) would
show whether the graceful degradation of the LLM family persists at tighter
budgets and whether the GAN family becomes competitive at looser ones. This
is left to future work.

\subsection{Computational Cost}
\label{sec:discussion:limit:compute}

The computational costs of the two paradigms differ by orders of magnitude
and were not equalised. The LLM generators, and especially the
differentially private variant, dominate: DP training through Opacus
materialises per-example gradients, which inflates memory consumption
relative to standard mini-batch SGD and necessitated the
effective-batch-size construction described in
\S\ref{sec:results:rq3:llm-toggle}. The GAN baseline, by contrast, trains
and samples cheaply. Comparisons in this thesis are made at a fixed privacy
budget rather than at fixed compute, and the utility rankings should be read
with that asymmetry in mind.

\subsection{Scope Reductions Versus the Proposal}
\label{sec:discussion:limit:scope}

Several measurements were narrowed from the proposal, in each case for a
stated methodological reason. Within the statistical-similarity branch,
Kullback-Leibler divergence was replaced by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov
statistic, which is non-parametric and avoids the probability-mass binning
that the former requires on continuous columns. The pairwise correlation
difference, originally specified as a single Frobenius norm, is reported both
as the per-column difference heatmaps and as the scalar mean used for
ranking. The correlation difference captures only linear inter-attribute
dependence; non-linear dependencies between attributes are not assessed in this
thesis, and are noted as future work
(Section~\ref{sec:conclusion:future:nonlinear}). Within the predictive branch,
the four-way train and test regime
was narrowed to the two cells that bound utility (Train-on-Real and
Train-on-Synthetic, both tested on real data), and the augmentation-based
and dimension-wise prediction evaluations were not run. Within the privacy
pillar, outlier linkability is defined through a centroid-distance criterion
rather than the DBSCAN clustering of \textcite{wang2019generating}. None of
these substitutions changes the dimension being measured. Each trades an
implementation detail for robustness or tractability.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussion:validity}

Following the standard classification, four categories of threat are
relevant. \emph{Internal validity} is limited by the use of a single train
and test split rather than cross-validation: the TSTR and privacy figures
are point estimates without variance bands, and a single random seed governs
both generation and the downstream classifiers. \emph{External validity} is
constrained by the conditions enumerated in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations}, namely the single dataset, the
single privacy budget, and the use of an English-language decoder-only model
family. The family rankings may not generalise beyond these conditions.
\emph{Construct validity} concerns whether the metrics measure what they
claim: the PATE-GAN result is a direct demonstration that nearest-neighbour
distance does not by itself measure privacy, because a generator far from the
real manifold scores as private while being useless. This is why the
framework interprets distance metrics only alongside utility, and why TSTR is
taken as a utility proxy that assumes the downstream task is representative.
\emph{Conclusion validity} is weakest for the attribute-inference cells,
where advantages are computed per attribute and the magnitudes involved (a
maximum of $+0.041$) are close to the resolution of the estimate. The
within-family DP effect on attribute inference should therefore be read as
suggestive rather than definitive.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:discussion:ethics}

The motivation for synthetic medical data is itself ethical, namely to
enable analysis without exposing patient records, but the practice carries
its own obligations. Even differentially private synthetic data is generated
from real patient records collected under specific consent and data-use
agreements. The synthetic output also does not inherit blanket permission to
be shared without regard to those terms. A particular hazard is false
confidence: a formal $\varepsilon$ guarantee bounds one specific privacy
notion and does not certify that every disclosure channel is closed, and, as
the PATE-GAN case shows, strong-looking privacy numbers can instead reflect
unusable output. Releasing a trained generator rather than the data shifts
but does not remove this risk, since the model encodes information about its
training set and remains susceptible to extraction attacks in
principle~\cite{Carlini2021Extracting}. Finally, synthetic data that
faithfully preserves the statistical structure of a clinical population also
reproduces the biases embedded in that population, so downstream models
trained on it warrant the same fairness scrutiny as models trained on the
real data.
